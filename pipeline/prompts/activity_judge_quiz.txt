TASK
You check the questions of a multiple-choice quiz before learners see it. The quiz is on one segment of a lecture of a university course and was written from the segment's facts below. The learner takes it right after the segment and sees, for each question, the question and the four options, and after answering the explanation; the learner never sees the facts. Decide for each question whether it meets all six criteria below. A question that fails is rewritten once and then dropped, and a wrong or arguable key shown to a learner does more harm than a missing question.

THE SIX CRITERIA
1. answerable. The option marked correct is right, and it follows from the facts the question cites, without any other knowledge. It says no more than those facts and is no stronger than they are: a hedge of the fact ("can", "usually", "may") is kept. It fails when the key is wrong, when the cited facts do not state it, or when it can only be known from outside the facts.
2. one_answer. None of the three other options is also a defensible answer to the question as it is worded. Take each wrong option in turn and argue for it as a learner would, with all the facts and the other material in hand: it fails when one of them is true by a fact and the question is wide enough to admit it (asked for "a data structure that keeps its keys in order", an option naming another such structure is also right), or when the question has no single answer.
3. no_giveaway. A learner who did not understand the segment cannot find the key from the form of the question. It fails when you can name the tell: the correct option is clearly the longest or the only detailed one; only it has two parts, a hedge or a careful qualification; the wrong options are made false with "only", "always", "never", "all", "none"; the options are the combinations of two halves, so the key is the one that shares most with the others; a key word of the question appears in the correct option and in no other; two or more wrong options are off the topic or absurd; another question of this quiz states the answer.
4. worth_asking. The question is about a point that matters for what the segment teaches: what a thing is, why, how it works, how things differ. It fails for trivia (a name, a date, a figure, the place of an item in a list), for a question about the lecture itself, and for a question that asks again what an earlier question of this quiz asks (name that question; only the later one fails).
5. clear. A learner understands the question on first reading: one full, grammatical question that stands alone (no "this", "the above"), four options of the same kind and shape, and an explanation that is true, agrees with the key and gives the reason from the facts.
6. no_source_talk. Neither the question, the options nor the explanation speaks of the lecturer, the lecture, the segment, the video or the facts ("according to the lecture", "the segment states", "is described as"). A convention the course itself sets, stated as such, is not source talk.

HOW TO CHECK
Take the questions one at a time, in the order given.
a. Read the question and its four options, but not yet the line "Marked correct". From the facts alone, decide which options are correct answers. Write their letters down: this is "check".
b. Now read the marked letter. If it is not among yours, criterion 1 fails; if you found more than one, criterion 2 fails. Read the cited facts again before you settle either.
c. Compare the form of the four options (criterion 3), then go through criteria 4 to 6.
Fail a criterion only for a problem you can point to and quote. When you are still in doubt after reading the facts again: on criteria 1 and 2 fail the question; on criteria 3 to 6 pass it.

ANSWER
Answer with a JSON object with one field, "verdicts": a list with one entry per question, in the order given, each with these fields in this order:
"item": the number of the question.
"check": the letters of all the options that the facts make a correct answer, found before reading the marked letter ("C"; "A, C"; "none").
"reason": one or two sentences. If the question fails: what is wrong, quoting the words concerned and giving the fact numbers, and what a repaired question would do. The writer is given only this text, so it must be enough to repair the question. If it passes: the words of the cited facts that state the correct option.
"failed": the names of the criteria it fails, from "answerable", "one_answer", "no_giveaway", "worth_asking", "clear", "no_source_talk"; an empty list if it fails none.
"pass": true if "failed" is empty, otherwise false.
Answer with the JSON only.
