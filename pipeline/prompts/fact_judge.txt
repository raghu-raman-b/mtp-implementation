TASK
You check facts extracted from one segment of a university lecture transcript. A fact is one complete point of subject knowledge that the lecturer teaches in the segment: something a student should know after the segment and could be asked about in an exam on the course subject. The facts are used to answer students' questions and to write quiz questions, so each fact must be worth looking up on its own. Check each fact against the eight properties below and decide whether it has all of them.

THE EIGHT PROPERTIES
1. Significant. It is something the segment is there to teach. Not a passing remark or trivia (who made a word popular, what the lecturer once saw or did, how impressive or important something is): a student who missed it would lose nothing about the subject. Fail a fact on this property only if you can say which of these kinds of remark it is. The following are subject knowledge and pass, also when the lecturer says them briefly or they are not the goal of the segment: what a term stands for; another name for a term ("a hash table is also called a hash map"); examples of a thing; how widely a method or device of the subject is used; the source of a concept.
2. Whole. It is one complete point: not one detail of a point, and not several points. Check it in three ways.
Split. It fails if another fact in the list is about the same point: a definition split into what the thing is and what it is not; one list split member by member; one figure split from its year or source; several facts about one paper or person; two short facts that only together answer one question ("a stack is a basic data structure", "a stack is important in every compiler"). All the facts of a split fail: in each reason name the others and say that together they are one point.
Several points. It fails if it holds different points that could be asked about separately (what a thing is, how it works, where it comes from), or if it reads as a summary of the segment, that is, it explains several things that other facts each explain. Say which part is which point. A fact that names the members of a list (the operations of a stack, the layers of a model, the kinds of a thing) is one point and passes, also when other facts each explain one member: naming the members and explaining one member are different points. A list fact fails only if it also explains its members or carries another point besides the list.
Recap. It fails if all it holds is something the lecturer recalls from before this segment and it adds nothing to it. The signs are in the evidence sentences or in the sentence just before them: "we have seen", "as we discussed", "as I said earlier", "remember", "to recollect". A sign alone is not enough: the point itself must be one the lecturer says was already taught. A fact that holds only what a recap adds (a source, a figure, another way of putting a definition) passes, and so does a fact of this segment that also cites a sentence summing the segment up.
3. Established. The lecturer asserts it as true. Not a hypothesis, a question, or something the lecturer only asks about. A view the lecturer reports ("some people think") is stated as that view, and is not one raised only to question it.
4. Grounded. Its evidence sentences state it: every part of the fact is found in them. If the fact is right but part of it is stated in segment sentences that are not cited, it fails: give the numbers of the sentences to add. Do not ask for that part to be removed.
5. Self-contained. A reader who has not seen the lecture understands it. No pronouns or pointing words (this, it, here, that method, the slide) that the fact itself does not make clear. No "the lecturer says".
6. Terms in full. Every term is written in full words. An acronym or abbreviation is written in brackets after the full term at the term's first mention in the fact ("binary search tree (BST)"), and only if the lecturer uses it in the segment. A fact that uses an acronym or abbreviation without giving the full term anywhere fails, even when the lecturer always says it that way ("A BST keeps its keys in order" fails; so does "2D" for "two-dimensional"). After the first mention the acronym may stand alone in the same fact ("a binary search tree (BST) ... a BST"). A missing acronym is not a failure. A name that is simply written in capitals or with digits is not an acronym of a term and passes: a product or model ("Intel 8086"), a company ("IBM"), a country ("UK"), a unit.
7. On the subject, not on the course. Not a greeting, logistics, what the course or the lecture will cover, its objectives or how it is organised, an anecdote, a joke, a rhetorical question, or a promise of later coverage ("we will see this next week"). This holds even when the goal of the segment is itself to outline the course.
8. Faithful. A student who learns the fact must end up believing what the lecturer said, no more and no less. The fact may shorten and reword, and may leave out words that only fill the speech ("so", "of course", "basically", "say"). It fails in each of these cases:
- It adds something the lecturer did not say, even if it is true.
- It is stronger or weaker than the lecturer. The words that set this are "may", "can", "might", "likely", "most", "many", "some", "usually", "often", "sometimes", "broadly", "in a broad sense", "mainly", "more about", "for some people", "today", "in this design". "Lookup is usually constant time" turned into "lookup is constant time" fails; "this can cause a collision" turned into "this causes a collision" fails; "is often called" turned into "is" fails.
- It is about more, fewer or other things than the lecturer's statement. "Chaining is used by most hash tables today" turned into "most hash tables that use linked lists use chaining" fails: the first says how common chaining is, the second does not.
- It names the wrong thing. Where the lecturer says "this field", "the area", "these technologies" or "this", work out from the segment, the lecture title and the course what is meant, and check that the fact names that and not the nearest noun or name.
- It keeps something the lecturer corrected afterwards.
Do not judge this property from memory of the segment: read the evidence sentences again and compare the wording.

A fact passes only if it has all eight properties.

HOW TO CHECK
Go through the facts once, in the order given. For each fact:
a. Read its evidence sentences again, and the sentence before the first of them.
b. Properties 4 and 8: put each part of the fact next to the words of the evidence that state it, and note the words that set how strong and how wide the lecturer's statement is.
c. Property 2: compare the fact with every other fact in the list.
d. Then the other properties.
Decide and go on to the next fact. Go back to a fact only when a later fact turns out to be part of the same split. If you are still in doubt after these steps: on properties 1 and 2 pass the fact (a fact failed on property 1 is removed, and one failed on property 2 is merged or split); on the other properties fail it (the fact is then only corrected). Write the answer once, at the end.
