TASK
You write open questions on one lecture of a university course: questions the learner answers in a few sentences of their own, after the lecture. A good open question asks the learner to explain, compare or reason: why one thing follows from another, how two concepts relate or differ, what a concept means for a case the lecture treats. Each has a reference answer built from the facts below; the learner's answer is compared with it and the learner then reads it as feedback.

{course}

THE LECTURE
{lecture_title}
Its goal: {goal}

THE FACTS
What the lecture teaches, then facts from earlier lectures that it builds on, one per line, each with its number in brackets.

{facts}

RELATIONSHIPS
How concepts of the lecture relate, each with the numbers of the facts that state it.

{relations}

CONCEPTS
The concepts you may name in "concepts":
{concepts}

THE QUESTIONS
- Write {count} questions, each about a different point. Stay at the lower number unless the lecture has material for more: a forced question is worse than one question fewer.
- What to ask. A question whose answer needs two or more facts put together: a reason, a comparison, a consequence, how a part serves the whole. The relationships above are links the lecture states: use them. A question that only asks to repeat one fact, a list or a figure is not an open question.
- The link must be in the facts. Ask "why" or "how" only when the facts give the reason or the way it works. If they only say that something is so, do not ask why it is so: the reference answer could then only repeat the facts without answering.
- Earlier lectures. Use a fact from an earlier lecture when this lecture really builds on it: it applies, extends or contrasts with that concept. Do not force the link: a question that ties this lecture to an early definition only because both name the same term ("How does X reflect the definition of Y?") teaches nothing.
- "key_facts": the numbers of the facts the reference answer is built from: at least two, and only facts the answer uses.
- "question": one question in one or two sentences, that stands alone: no "this", "the above" or "in this lecture", and things are named in full. It follows one thread: not two questions joined by "and" ("How ..., and why ...?"). It does not contain its own answer, and it does not presuppose something odd ("Why can X be understood as ...?").
- "reference_answer": two to four sentences, at most about eighty words. The first sentence answers the question directly; the others give the support from the key facts. Every part of the question is answered. From the key facts only: add nothing from your own knowledge, keep the hedges of the facts ("usually", "can"), and no "the lecture says".
- "concepts": the concepts the question is mainly about (usually two to four), by their names exactly as listed above.
