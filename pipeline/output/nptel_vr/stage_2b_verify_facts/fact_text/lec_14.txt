[14/S0/F0] Non-hand input techniques in virtual reality (VR) consist of head tracking, eye tracking, full-body tracking, and sound-based tracking.
[14/S0/F1] Full-body tracking is a non-hand input method in virtual reality (VR) that tracks full-body movement, more than the head, hands, and eyes.
[14/S0/F2] Full-body tracking can add to the illusion of self-embodiment and social presence in a virtual world because its output can show the user a fuller representation of themselves.
[14/S0/F3] There are two broad approaches to full-body tracking: a motion capture suit (MOCAP) and camera-based tracking.
[14/S0/F4] In a motion capture suit (MOCAP) approach to full-body tracking, sensors in the suit capture and transmit raw movement data, which are combined and converted into full-body position and movement; for example, the Perception Neuron suit uses straps with inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors on the legs, hands, arms, and head.
[14/S0/F5] In camera-based full-body tracking, multiple surrounding cameras observe an actor wearing a suit with fiducial markers, typically reflective markers, and infer the actor's overall body movement from the markers' movement; OptiTrack and Microsoft Kinect are examples of such camera-based full-body tracking systems.
[14/S0/F6] Sound-based tracking is a non-hand input technique in virtual reality (VR) that captures voice, speech, and non-speech sound for interaction using simple microphones or microphone arrays, which are sets of microphones that capture sound in a 360-degree environment.
[14/S1/F0] In a virtual reality (VR) system, the processing part or computer is a device that processes data from input sensors and, based on that processing, activates or actuates output devices.
[14/S1/F1] The processing element or computer in a virtual reality (VR) system may be a standard personal computer (PC), a high-end workstation with a high-quality graphics processing unit (GPU), a distributed computer system interconnected through a high-speed communication network, or a smartphone, and its selection depends on the application, the target users, the assumed input and output devices and their availability, and the required level of immersion.
[14/S1/F2] The major components of a computer used as the processing element in a virtual reality (VR) system are the graphics processing unit (GPU), the central processing unit (CPU), and random access memory (RAM).
[14/S1/F3] A graphics processing unit (GPU) is used to speed up the rendering process because of its inherent parallel processing abilities and the inherent parallelism in the rendering process, and it is therefore very useful for rendering complex graphics and complex scenes that are part of realistic virtual reality (VR) experiences.
[14/S1/F4] Multi-GPU-enabled systems are typically used for building virtual reality (VR) applications, and a good-quality graphics processing unit (GPU)-enabled graphics card or GPU-enabled environment is preferable, especially for rendering.
[14/S1/F5] In virtual reality (VR) processing, multicore processors greatly increase computational capacity compared with non-multicore or unicore processors, and nowadays processors are multicore by default.
[14/S1/F6] Modern central processing unit (CPU) processors are quite powerful, but they have many other tasks to do, so a CPU-only virtual reality (VR) processing system with minimal graphics processing unit (GPU) support can have performance issues, and GPU support is preferable.
[14/S1/F7] An extended reality (XR) engine is middleware required to support, develop, and render virtual reality (VR) experiences.
[14/S1/F8] Each extended reality (XR) engine has its own minimum random access memory (RAM) requirement, and at least 8 GB of RAM is typically preferable in a virtual reality (VR) system's hardware for a good user experience.
[14/S2/F0] Output devices for virtual reality (VR) experience include visual displays, audio output, haptics, motion platforms and treadmills, and other sensory outputs that stimulate the taste and smell sensory organs, namely the tongue and nose.
[14/S2/F1] Other sensory output systems for taste and smell in virtual reality (VR) are premature, not widely used, available, or popular, and are mostly confined to highly specialized environments or research domains.
[14/S2/F2] Visual displays are displays meant to stimulate the visual sense, that is the eyes.
[14/S2/F3] The three broad types of visual displays are head-mounted displays (HMDs), which are wearable and mobile, world-fixed displays, which are not wearable and are stationary, and regular displays, which are mobile or can be stationary.
[14/S2/F4] Although head-mounted displays (HMDs) can be used as input to track head motion, their primary use is as a display device.
[14/S2/F5] A head-mounted display (HMD) is a sophisticated piece of equipment that integrates electronic, optical, mechanical, and audio components within its package.
[14/S2/F6] A head-mounted display (HMD) places images directly in front of the user's eyes using one or two small screens, with one screen providing monoscopic viewing and two screens providing stereoscopic viewing, and stereoscopic HMDs are more common than monoscopic HMDs.
[14/S2/F7] Head-mounted displays (HMDs) can be further categorized into three types: non-see-through, optical see-through, and video see-through.
[14/S2/F8] Non-see-through HMDs are typically used in virtual reality (VR), optical see-through HMDs are typically used in augmented reality (AR), and video see-through HMDs are typically used in augmented virtuality, in which real-world images or videos appear in real time within an artificial or virtual environment.
[14/S2/F9] A world-fixed display renders graphics onto a surface or screen that is fixed and does not move with the user's head; the display is not moving, while the user can move within a physical space.
[14/S2/F10] World-fixed displays range from standard monitors to displays that completely surround the user, typically called a cave.
[14/S2/F11] The fish tank display, developed in 1993, is an early world-fixed virtual reality (VR) display that uses a fixed cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitor as the output and an optical head tracker and a haptic device as inputs, so that the stereo perspective view of a 3D scene changes as the user's head moves.
[14/S2/F12] A more recent world-fixed display example is the surround screen, or cave system, which typically has three to six large screens around the user for visual immersion and is usually driven by a single or group of powerful graphics engines and requires elaborate tracking of the user's movements.
[14/S2/F13] Regular displays, which can be mobile or stationary, refer to common tablets or smartphones, are not specialized, are typically relevant for non-immersive virtual reality (VR), and are mostly used for augmented reality (AR).
[14/S3/F0] Audio output is an output device typically used in a virtual reality (VR) setup, and it can provide specialized three-dimensional (3D) audio for a sense of where sounds are coming from, with speakers fixed in space or moving with the head, and headphones are preferred for a fully immersive system because the sound source can change with head movement.
[14/S3/F1] Haptic rendering systems are outputs that activate touch sensation and explore touch perception, and haptic outputs can be categorized as passive versus active, tactile versus proprioceptive, and self-grounded versus world-grounded devices.
[14/S3/F2] Passive haptic devices are static real-world physical objects that match the shape of virtual objects and are not controlled by the computer, whereas active haptic devices are physical objects or physical feedback controlled by the computer that provide a sense of touch when interacting with virtual objects.
[14/S3/F3] Tactile haptics are haptic devices that provide touch sensations through the skin, and they include vibrotactile stimulation, which uses mechanical vibration of the skin, and electrotactile stimulation, which uses electrodes that pass current through the skin.
[14/S3/F4] Proprioceptive force-based haptics use forces that activate joints or muscles and provide a sense of limb movement and muscular resistance by providing corresponding signals to the muscles and joints.
[14/S3/F5] Self-grounded haptic devices are worn or held by the user and move with the user, with handheld controllers as examples, whereas world-grounded haptic devices are physically attached to the real world, cannot be moved, and can provide the sense of fully solid objects that do not move.
[14/S3/F6] Motion platforms are hardware output devices that move the entire body to produce a sense of physical motion and gravity, conveying a sense of orientation, vibration, acceleration, and jerks.
[14/S3/F7] Use of motion platforms may result in motion sickness, which can be reduced by decreasing the conflict between visual motion and the perceived motion in the real world.
[14/S3/F8] Motion platforms can be active, meaning they are controlled by computer simulation, or passive, meaning they are controlled by the user with no direct computer control, and passive platforms are likely to result in less motion sickness than active platforms.
[14/S3/F9] Treadmills are output devices that provide the sense of walking or running while staying in one place by providing different sorts of input to the feet or legs.
[14/S3/F10] Omnidirectional treadmills are common in virtual reality systems and can be active, with computer-controlled mechanically moving parts that create the sensation of walking or moving, or passive, containing no computer-controlled mechanically moving parts and controlled by the user.
