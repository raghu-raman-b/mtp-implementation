[46/S0/F0] In experiment design, the selected participants form a sample or subset of all potential users, and the subset must be chosen carefully to be representative of the whole user group; otherwise the conclusions drawn from the subset are not valid or reliable.
[46/S0/F1] A homogeneous user group is a user group whose members have similarities such as the same age group and similar educational, socio-economic, and/or cultural backgrounds; using a homogeneous subset is acceptable when the system is for such a homogeneous group, but it is not reliable for drawing conclusions for a heterogeneous user group.
[46/S0/F2] In experiment design, a user profile is a set of well-defined user profile characteristics or entries; the user is first defined by such a user profile, and experiment participants are selected only on the basis of that profile.
[46/S0/F3] Older people may have different typing behavior from teenagers, and regular computer users are likely to have different typing speeds from infrequent users of computers, so such user characteristics must be considered when choosing experiment participants based on user profiles.
[46/S1/F0] For a controlled empirical experiment, the number of participants is generally assumed to be between 12 and 25 to draw reliable conclusions, more than 25 can be used, and below 12 the conclusions may not be reliable and may change if the number is increased.
[46/S1/F1] In a controlled experiment evaluating an interface, users perform tasks, and the task performance data collected from them are mostly objective data that are analyzed to draw conclusions about the interface.
[46/S1/F2] In a controlled experiment evaluating an interface, only a small subset of all tasks supported by the system can be used because of less time or practicality, and that subset must be chosen carefully, otherwise the observed data may not be useful for drawing a reliable conclusion about the overall usability of the interface.
[46/S1/F3] For an interface, a representative task set is a small, well-defined subset of tasks that are used by everybody most of the time, namely the tasks that potential users most frequently or commonly use when the interface is deployed in practice, and in an empirical study one or more tasks from this set should ideally be chosen so that the collected data and drawn conclusions are reliable.
[46/S1/F4] For a typing task used to compare typing interfaces, a random piece of text is not a representative task, whereas representative tasks are text strings chosen based on certain metrics and measures that indirectly encompass activities spanning the entire interface and the users' typing skills.
[46/S2/F0] In an experimental study, tasks can be assigned using a within-subject (repeated measures) design, in which every participant performs the tasks corresponding to all test conditions, or a between-subjects design, in which the tasks for all test conditions are divided and distributed among subsets of participants.
[46/S2/F1] Within-subject designs are easier to manage than between-subject designs, but choosing between them depends on circumstances, such as the availability of participants and resources, which may force a between-subject design.
[46/S2/F2] In a within-subject design, the practice effect is an improvement in task performance from repeated practice; it is also called a confounding variable and confounds the analysis by adding noise to the data, because performance gains may be due to practice rather than to the interface.
[46/S2/F3] To reduce the practice effect in a within-subject design, the task sequence can be randomized or systematically varied; statistically, these random or systematic variations are supposed to nullify the practice effect.
[46/S2/F4] A Latin square design systematically varies the task sequence allocated to each participant, for example by shifting a numbered task sequence one position for each successive participant.
[46/S3/F0] In the system implementation process, stage 4, data analysis, is the stage in which empirical data from an experiment are processed and tested for statistical significance before drawing conclusions about the efficacy of a design, such as its text-entry speed.
[46/S3/F1] In empirical interface studies, the data are obtained from a sample of users rather than the actual population, while the conclusions of interest are about the larger population, such as any user and any text-entry interface.
[46/S3/F2] A straightforward conclusion from experimental data can be misleading because an observed result may be due to chance, and if it is due to chance, changing the participants or conditions may produce different data and a different conclusion.
[46/S3/F3] A statistical significance test indicates whether an observed data difference is reliable by showing whether the difference is due to chance or to the tested conditions; a statistically significant difference is not due to chance, and a non-significant difference is due to chance.
[46/S3/F4] In comparing two text-entry interfaces, the analysis uses the mean text-entry speed of each group and concerns the relative difference between the means rather than the absolute values.
[46/S3/F5] In comparing two text-entry interfaces, a higher mean text-entry speed alone may be due to chance, so a statistically significant difference between the means is required to conclude that one design is better in speed or efficiency.
[46/S4/F0] The method for performing a statistical significance test depends on the design of the experiment and the nature of the input data.
[46/S4/F1] The result of a statistical significance test can include the absolute difference in group means, the two-tailed p-value, the T value, the degrees of freedom, and whether the difference is statistically significant, and such results are typically reported, for example, as t = 1.70, p < 0.05, statistically significant, which is the standard way of representing any statistical significance test.
[46/S4/F2] In a statistical significance test, such as a paired sample t-test, the p-value is the probability of obtaining the data by chance, and a lower p-value means the result is less likely to have been obtained by chance.
[46/S4/F3] There are many statistical significance tests, which fall into two groups, parametric tests and non-parametric tests.
[46/S4/F4] For an experimental design with a nominal or categorical scale of measurement and one factor having two levels, the non-parametric chi-square test is used for a between-subject design, and the non-parametric McNemar's test is used for a within-subject design.
[46/S4/F5] For an experimental design with one factor having two levels and no other condition mentioned, a between-subject design can use the parametric independent samples t-test or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, and a within-subject design can use the parametric paired sample t-test or the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
[46/S4/F6] For between-subject designs, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is used for one factor with more than two levels and factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) is used for two or more factors each having two or more levels, and these two tests are parametric tests; the Kruskal-Wallis test can also be used for a between-subject design with one factor having more than two levels.
[46/S4/F7] For a within-subject design with one factor having three or more levels, or for a within-subject design with two or more factors each having two or more levels, the parametric repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the non-parametric Friedman test can be used.
