[36/S0/F0] Contextual inquiry is a usability requirement-gathering method that observes targeted or specified groups of users in their natural work setting to understand how they behave and what they do.
[36/S0/F1] Contextual inquiry can be performed in two broad modes: active mode, where the observer is physically present in the environment where the users are doing their tasks, and passive mode, where no observer is present, user activities are recorded or videotaped and later observed by the observer, and issues are clarified through interaction with the users.
[36/S0/F2] In genuine contextual inquiry, the observer must act as an apprentice or learner and treat users as masters or teachers, the master-apprentice model, learning how users behave instead of advising or teaching them how to behave; such advice is prohibited.
[36/S1/F0] The five stages of contextual inquiry are plan, initiate, execute, close, and reflect, in that order, and they provide a systematic way of carrying out the inquiry process.
[36/S1/F1] The plan stage of contextual inquiry involves identifying the goals of observation, gathering knowledge about the task domain, planning arrangements for recording observations, identifying the users and the date, time, and place of the observation, and, as a helpful practice, scripting and rehearsing the procedure.
[36/S1/F2] The initiate stage of contextual inquiry involves obtaining all necessary permissions from relevant authorities before observing employees in their workplace and opening communication with end users to remove anxieties that the observation may lead to something bad for them, so that they behave naturally.
[36/S1/F3] The execute stage of contextual inquiry is the stage in which the actual inquiry takes place, through performing observations and recording data.
[36/S1/F4] The close stage of contextual inquiry ideally involves formally closing by sending thank-you notes or a message of gratitude to the users and possibly the authorities, because such users may be useful for future activities such as usability testing.
[36/S1/F5] The reflect stage of contextual inquiry is the stage in which the observer or experimental team analyzes the data collected during the execute stage and identifies the design goals.
[36/S1/F6] The affinity diagram method is a technique used in the reflect stage of contextual inquiry to arrange the collected data so that patterns can be found more easily, leading to the design specification of the design goals.
[36/S2/F0] The affinity diagram method is a method for analyzing data collected through contextual inquiry, and it consists of five steps: generate ideas, display ideas, sort ideas into groups, create a header or name for each group, and draw the finished diagram.
[36/S2/F1] In the affinity diagram method, the generated ideas are the observers' final notes from contextual inquiry, possibly refined in later processing, and displaying the ideas means putting those notes together in a place such as a wall or a board.
[36/S2/F2] Sorting ideas into groups in the affinity diagram method is done to find patterns among ideas that may look random, by moving similar ideas into the same group so that meaningful conclusions can be drawn.
[36/S2/F3] In the affinity diagram method, a group header is a name or identifier assigned to a group after the ideas have been sorted, and it refers to the pattern that binds the ideas within that group; the header is not present in the initial ideas.
[36/S2/F4] In the class note-taking application example, the 19 affinity-diagram ideas are organized into six groups with the headers navigation in notebook, media content, navigation in notes, note organization, text formatting, and storage and privacy, containing 3, 5, 2, 2, 4, and 3 ideas respectively.
[36/S2/F5] After the finished affinity diagram is drawn, reflection means deciding what to do with the data and formulating a set of design recommendations based on the data.
[36/S2/F6] Design recommendations or guidelines derived from contextual inquiry are informed design choices because they are produced by analyzing the collected inquiry data, whereas guidelines created without contextual inquiry are uninformed design choices that do not ensure that users' needs and expectations are taken into account.
[36/S2/F7] A design recommendation such as providing an erase-content facility in the class note-taking application for correcting mistakes is a requirement, not a prescription for implementation; it states what must be done to make the system usable, and it does not specify how to implement it.
[36/S3/F0] In the calendar app affinity diagram example, the observations that all users set reminders and that three out of five users used a mobile app to check the day and date and set a reminder for assignment submission are grouped under the header reminder setting.
[36/S3/F1] In the calendar app affinity diagram example, the five contextual inquiry (CI) observations that do not relate to reminder setting, namely checking upcoming deadlines, checking the date and then the timetable, checking meeting dates, checking meeting schedules on a particular day, and checking lecture schedules on a particular day, were first grouped under the header work scheduling and then refined into the headers overall scheduling and day-wise scheduling, giving a finished diagram with three groups: reminder setting, overall scheduling, and day-wise scheduling.
[36/S3/F2] In an affinity diagram, there is no unique way to group the observations; different groupings are possible.
