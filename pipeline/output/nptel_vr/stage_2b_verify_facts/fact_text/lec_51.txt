[51/S0/F0] The natural walking model for a system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tour is intended to provide a realistic walking experience by displaying a visual speed, or optic flow (the rate at which scenes change in the virtual world), that matches the user's perceived instantaneous walking speed, producing the perception of movement.
[51/S0/F1] The natural walking model for a system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tour does not provide anything related to experience optimization; it can only be used to enhance presence in the virtual world.
[51/S0/F2] The approach to optimizing virtual reality (VR) navigation experience that uses the natural walking model addresses cyber sickness by controlling the duration of exposure in the virtual world.
[51/S0/F3] In a virtual reality (VR) environment, cyber sickness is correlated with exposure duration, with the degree of the symptoms rising proportionally as the VR session duration increases, so if possible, shorter exposure duration can help eliminate or at least minimize the onset of cyber sickness.
[51/S1/F0] The duration of virtual reality (VR) exposure depends on the size of the virtual world, the travelling speed, the travelling time, and the interaction time spent inspecting, selecting, or manipulating virtual objects.
[51/S1/F1] A larger virtual reality (VR) environment leads to a longer duration of VR exposure because the user needs to cover a larger geographical area.
[51/S1/F2] Increasing the virtual travel or navigation speed reduces the duration of exposure to a virtual world because a higher speed lowers the time needed to travel and explore the entire geographical area.
[51/S1/F3] To provide a realistic walking experience in a virtual world, virtual travel speed cannot be modified arbitrarily; an arbitrary speed imposed on the user while navigating may not be a good idea.
[51/S1/F4] Teleportation-type movement in a virtual world can be the best choice for reducing overall exposure duration because it provides instantaneous movement between two points at maximum speed, but it is not a natural movement and may not always be a good approach when naturalness is required.
[51/S1/F5] Manipulating the virtual terrain or adjusting the distances between points of interest (POIs) can reduce travel time and therefore exposure time, but arbitrary terrain changes prevent users from perceiving the true size and feel of the real space and can hamper their mental map and wayfinding in the virtual world.
[51/S1/F6] In a purely system-automated or system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tool, users do not interact with virtual objects, so the interaction time required to interact with virtual objects can be ignored.
[51/S1/F7] A semi-interactive tour in virtual reality (VR) is a system-controlled navigation scenario in which travel is automatic, users may interact with objects after a navigation task is complete, and interaction is limited to a small set of objects at points of interest (POIs), while users cannot interact with other objects in the virtual environment.
[51/S2/F0] In a semi-interactive virtual tour, interaction time at each point of interest (POI) can be limited to an estimate in advance of the maximum time an average user needs to interact with that POI, after which the system automatically forces the user to move to another POI.
[51/S2/F1] In a semi-interactive virtual reality (VR) tour, the duration of exposure can be optimized, under a limit on interaction time, by finding an optimum path that either minimizes the total navigation and interaction time required to experience all points of interest (POIs) or maximizes the number of POIs experienced within a fixed time limit for navigation and interaction.
[51/S2/F2] Optimizing the duration of exposure in a virtual reality (VR) tour is expected to optimize the prevalence and onset of cyber sickness, because exposure duration and cyber sickness are directly related.
[51/S3/F0] The virtual world part of a virtual experience is represented as a weighted graph G = (V, E), where V is the vertex set of all points of interest (POIs) and E is the set of weighted edges between POIs, representing a top view of the virtual world.
[51/S3/F1] The graph G used to represent the virtual world need not be complete, because in a realistic environment there may not always be a direct path from one place to another.
[51/S3/F2] For every pair of vertices v1 and v2 in the vertex set V, the assigned edge weight is the Euclidean distance between v1 and v2 if they are directly connected, and a very large number represented by the infinity symbol if they are not directly connected.
[51/S3/F3] In the mapping of the virtual world to the graph, each point of interest (POI) is a node, while a single object or structure is not necessarily a single node because it may contain multiple points of interest.
[51/S3/F4] The optimum travel path problem covering all points of interest (POIs) is to find a path P in the given graph G that covers all vertices in V, which are the POIs, and finishes in the least possible time while maintaining a realistic speed throughout, so that the user gets a realistic feeling.
[51/S4/F0] The first stage of the travel-time algorithm is to compute the time to travel between two directly connected nodes using the natural walking-speed model.
[51/S4/F1] In the natural walking-speed model, perceived instantaneous walking speed is the visual gain of 2.1 multiplied by the actual instantaneous realistic walking speed (V act).
[51/S4/F2] Because speed is dx/dt, the instantaneous time dt for a distance element dx is dx divided by 2.1 times the actual instantaneous realistic walking speed (V act), and the total travel time over a distance D between directly connected nodes is the integral from 0 to D of dx/(2.1 V act).
[51/S4/F3] In the natural walking-speed model, f is the instantaneous step frequency and h is the height of the user.
[51/S4/F4] After solving the integral with simplifications, rearrangements, and Fourier-series expansion up to n0, the travel-time estimate tau D for a directly connected vertex pair under the natural walking model is D/V naught times (3 - (16/pi^2) * sum_{k=1}^{n0} 1/(2k-1)^2).
[51/S4/F5] V naught is a constant for a given height h, equal to 2.1 h^2 divided by (0.157 * 1.72^2 * 1.8^2), where h is the user's height.
[51/S5/F0] For a path P in a graph that contains n vertices, the total travel time for P is the sum over i = 1 to n - 1 of the edge weight epsilon(v_i, v_{i+1}) between consecutive vertices, and epsilon(v_i, v_{i+1}) is the Euclidean distance between that pair of vertices.
[51/S5/F1] In the travel-time minimisation problem for a graph, the objective is to find a path P that contains all vertices of the graph with the minimum total travel time, and this problem is a variation of the travelling salesman problem (TSP).
[51/S5/F2] The travelling salesman problem (TSP) asks, given a complete graph, for the shortest possible path that visits each node exactly once and returns to the starting node, where the shortest path is defined by the total weight of the edges in the path.
[51/S5/F3] The graph used for the travel-time minimisation problem is not assumed to be a complete graph, so there may be no Hamiltonian path in that graph, where a Hamiltonian path is a path that covers all nodes and visits each node exactly once.
[51/S5/F4] For any two vertices in the graph used for the travel-time minimisation problem that are not directly connected, a hypothetical edge is introduced for computation, and the modified edge weight epsilon dash(v1, v2) is d(v1, v2), the Euclidean distance, if v1 and v2 are directly connected, or the shortest path length between v1 and v2 if they are not directly connected, where that shortest path length is the sum of the edge weights in the shortest path.
[51/S5/F5] The shortest path lengths used for the hypothetical edges are well-defined because the graph used for the travel-time minimisation problem is connected.
[51/S6/F0] The algorithm takes the original graph G, with vertex set V and edge set epsilon, as input and produces the optimal time T and the path P of the minimum-time path that covers all vertices in G.
[51/S6/F1] The algorithm first converts the original graph G into a complete graph G' by keeping every directly connected edge unchanged and, for each pair of vertices that is not directly connected, adding a hypothetical edge whose length is the shortest path between those vertices in G.
[51/S6/F2] After the graph is complete, the algorithm replaces the Euclidean-distance edge weights with the travel time between each pair of vertices, producing a complete time-weighted graph G'' whose edge weights are travel times rather than Euclidean distances.
[51/S6/F3] The complete time-weighted graph G'' can be solved with a standard travelling salesman problem (TSP) solver, without needing a new algorithm, and the solver returns the optimal time tau_opt and the optimal path p_opt for that modified graph.
[51/S6/F4] Because the travelling salesman problem (TSP) solver's optimal path p_opt may contain hypothetical edges that are not in the original graph G, the algorithm post-processes p_opt into p_dash_opt by checking each path edge and replacing each edge whose endpoints were not directly connected in G with the shortest path between those endpoints in G.
[51/S6/F5] The minimum travel time to cover all vertices, as computed by the algorithm that maps the original graph to a travelling salesman problem (TSP) instance, may still exceed the cybersickness time threshold when the terrain or virtual environment is large, so its purpose may be defeated in such cases.
[51/S7/F0] There are two approaches to minimizing the possibility of cybersickness in a virtual world: finding a path that covers all points of interest in minimum time, and finding a path that covers the maximum possible points of interest within a given time limit.
[51/S7/F1] In the minimum-time approach, the virtual world is represented as a graph whose nodes are points of interest, whose directly connected edges are weighted by the Euclidean distance between the corresponding points of interest, and whose graph need not be complete.
[51/S7/F2] The problem of finding the minimum-duration travel that covers all points of interest in the virtual-world graph is mapped to the travelling salesman problem (TSP), a standard classical problem.
[51/S7/F3] In the minimum-time approach, travel times between pairs of vertices are computed using the natural walking speed model, so if the vertices are directly connected the model gives the travel time between them, and if they are not directly connected a hypothetical edge representing the shortest path between them is introduced and the travel time along that shortest path is computed using the model.
[51/S7/F4] In the minimum-time approach, the computed travel times become the weights of a complete graph formed by adding hypothetical edges, and this complete graph is given as input to a travelling salesman problem (TSP) solver, which outputs the optimum time and optimal path.
