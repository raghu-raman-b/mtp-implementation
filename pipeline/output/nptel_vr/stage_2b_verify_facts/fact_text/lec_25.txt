[25/S0/F0] In an Extended Reality (XR) system, interaction design and modeling, which relate to input and give the user ease of interacting with the system, and perceptual design and modeling, which relate to output and make the output appropriately sensed, understood, or perceived by the user, are both important for usability.
[25/S0/F1] In an Extended Reality (XR) system, the interaction and the perceptual stimuli, the latter being the output generated by the system, are designed to create the best possible illusion of reality, not actual reality, in order to enhance presence, which is a sense of reality as if the user is in the real world.
[25/S0/F2] Creating the best possible illusion of reality is very crucial for virtual reality (VR) because VR aims for total immersion in a virtual environment, whereas in augmented reality (AR) or mixed reality (MR) immersion is partial and the real world remains viewable or experienced, so the illusion is less significant but not totally insignificant.
[25/S1/F0] A virtual reality (VR) system is designed to create interactions that make the user feel immersed, and the amount of presence and the quality of the overall experience depend on fooling the user's perceptual system into believing the virtual system is the familiar real world.
[25/S1/F1] Human bodies and sensory organs are designed to perceive the real world, not a virtual world, and applying artificial stimulation in virtual reality affects biological mechanisms that evolved over a long period in a natural environment.
[25/S1/F2] Human perception is formed by the combination of physiology and experience stored in long-term memory.
[25/S1/F3] Virtual reality generally provides the brain with sensory signals that are inconsistent with the user's real-life experiences and behavior.
[25/S1/F4] One possible outcome of generally inconsistent virtual reality stimulation is that the body adapts to the new stimuli, making the user unaware of any flaws in the system and causing the user to believe that the virtual system is the real world rather than an artificial system.
[25/S1/F5] Another possible outcome of generally inconsistent virtual reality stimulation is that the body may not be able to adapt, causing increased fatigue or headaches, and this non-adaptation may lead to cyber sickness, that is, dizziness, nausea, or a large set of different symptoms experienced while using virtual reality systems.
[25/S1/F6] An understanding of human perception is needed to design a better virtual reality (VR) system or to identify and resolve flaws in a given system.
[25/S2/F0] Perceptual psychology is the science of understanding how the brain converts sensory stimulation, received by sensors from the real world, into perceived phenomena.
[25/S2/F1] Having a little bit of knowledge of perceptual psychology is always advisable for interactive systems in general and is even more important for extended reality (XR) systems because the goal of extended reality systems is to explicitly extend the user's deeply embedded idea of reality.
[25/S2/F2] Perceptual psychology provides guidelines for building better hardware and for understanding the effects of an extended reality (XR) system on the user by answering questions such as how far away an object appears in a given virtual world, how much video resolution is needed to avoid seeing pixels in a virtual world or virtual object, how many frames per second are enough to perceive motion as continuous, whether the user's head appears at the proper height in the virtual world for the point of view that should correspond to the user's height, where virtual sound appears to come from, why a user feels nauseated, why one system experience is more tiring than another, and what presence is.
[25/S2/F3] Perceptual psychology requires knowledge of the basic physiology of the human body, including sense organs, neural pathways, and how they are connected to the brain; the key theories and insights of experimental perceptual psychology; and the interference of an engineered extended reality (XR) system with common perceptual processes, with its implications or side effects.
[25/S3/F0] The term sensor has two related but structurally different meanings: in a device or system, a sensor is a device that converts an energy source into electrical signals in a circuit; in the human body, human bodily sensors are body organs that convert a stimulus into neural impulses.
[25/S3/F1] The human senses classified by stimulus, receptor, and sense organ are vision (electromagnetic energy, photoreceptors, eye), auditory (air pressure waves, mechanoreceptors, ear), touch (tissue distortion, mechanoreceptors and thermoreceptors, skin and muscles), balance (gravity or acceleration, mechanoreceptors, vestibular organs that act as sense organs), taste (chemical composition, chemoreceptors, mouth), and smell (chemical composition, chemoreceptors, nose).
[25/S3/F2] Among the six human senses, balance is a sense for which there is no specific organ, unlike the other five well-known senses.
[25/S4/F0] Sensory system selectivity is the phenomenon in which the receptors in a sense organ are bodily sensors that target particular stimuli and are activated only when a specific type of stimulus arrives.
[25/S4/F1] The eyes contain photoreceptors, which are activated by electromagnetic energy in the frequency range of visible light and therefore target only visible light.
[25/S4/F2] The auditory, touch, and balance senses involve mechanoreceptors that sense motion, vibration, or gravitational force.
[25/S4/F3] The sense of touch additionally involves thermoreceptors that detect changes in temperature.
[25/S4/F4] The balance sense helps a person know which way the head is oriented, including sensing the upward direction.
[25/S4/F5] Taste and smell rely on chemoreceptors that provide signals based on the chemical composition of matter, on the tongue for taste and in the nasal passage for smell.
[25/S5/F0] Some knowledge of perceptual psychology is needed to understand the role of human factors in extended reality systems.
[25/S5/F1] The physiology of the senses is a part of perceptual psychology.
[25/S5/F2] The six senses are vision, auditory, balance, taste, smell, and touch.
[25/S5/F3] For each of the six senses, receptors are part of the sensory organs, and these receptors receive stimuli depending on the sensory organ.
[25/S5/F4] Different types of receptors include photoreceptors, chemoreceptors, and mechanoreceptors.
[25/S5/F5] In the human body, the sensory organs are the hardware of sensing.
