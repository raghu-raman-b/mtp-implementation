[43/S0/F0] Prototypes can be used in different ways: as throwaway prototypes, incremental prototypes, and evolutionary prototypes.
[43/S0/F1] Throwaway prototypes are prototypes that are used for quick evaluation and then discarded.
[43/S0/F2] Low-fidelity cardboard mockups and paper sketches are mainly suitable for throwaway prototypes because they are less costly and discarding them does not incur significant cost, and throwaway prototypes should be easy and fast to create, otherwise working with them becomes expensive.
[43/S0/F3] Incremental prototypes are created when the system design divides the system into units or modules, each module is separately prototyped and tested, and the modules are then integrated into the overall system.
[43/S0/F4] Evolutionary prototypes are created by prototyping and testing the whole system from the beginning, starting with a simpler version, refining it, adding more functionalities, and altering it based on testing results until it becomes the final product, rather than prototyping separate modules and combining them.
[43/S1/F0] Quick evaluation of prototypes, also known as expert evaluation, is a technique for quickly and cheaply evaluating a prototype at an early stage of design, unlike a robust evaluation technique, which typically involves suitably skilled evaluators, resources, and an evaluation setup.
[43/S1/F1] The prerequisites for performing quick or expert evaluation are at least a low-fidelity prototype, with higher-quality prototypes being better, and a team of evaluators.
[43/S1/F2] Quick evaluation is a usability test, but although the primary condition of usability testing is to involve users, the evaluation team for quick or expert evaluation is primarily the design team itself or skilled designers with knowledge and skill in the problem domain, and may optionally include a few end users.
[43/S1/F3] Quick or expert evaluation can rely on design-team members or domain experts rather than end users because they are assumed to have user-like experience, their feedback likely reflects users, and engaging them is less complicated, quicker, and cheaper than engaging a full evaluation team with end users.
[43/S1/F4] The evaluation team for expert evaluation should have at least three to five members; more members are welcome, but three to five is sufficient.
[43/S1/F5] In expert evaluation, each evaluator is presented with the prototype, evaluates it independently, and produces a report containing the usability issues perceived by that evaluator; the design team or team lead then combines these reports into a final list of usability issues.
[43/S2/F0] Cognitive walkthrough is a quick expert evaluation technique and usability inspection method in which usability problems are identified by inspecting a prototype.
[43/S2/F1] A cognitive walkthrough requires at least a low-fidelity vertical prototype, a prototype that supports several tasks and may be low- or medium-fidelity, and an evaluation team of three to five members; a high-fidelity prototype is more welcome but involves more time and cost.
[43/S2/F2] A low-fidelity prototype for a cognitive walkthrough can be made as simple paper prototypes using the storyboarding approach, in which each prototype is a series of sketches depicting screen changes after each interaction, that is, a storyboard.
[43/S2/F3] In a cognitive walkthrough, a scenario or task description is created for the evaluators, and the task described in the scenario is converted into interface-level tasks, which are the actions that must be performed in the prototype to accomplish that task; the evaluators then perform those actions with the prototype.
[43/S2/F4] Usability questions are framed before a cognitive walkthrough to reflect usability issues, and evaluators use them as guidelines to identify problems users are likely to face while performing the tasks.
[43/S2/F5] For each usability question, evaluators must give a detailed report rather than a simple yes or no answer, explaining where they felt difficulty in the interface or interaction and why.
[43/S2/F6] The evaluators' reports are collected, combined, and analyzed at the end of the evaluation session to identify broader usability issues.
[43/S3/F0] Heuristic evaluation is an evaluation technique in which evaluators assess a presented prototype by ticking items on a checklist of heuristics, without a task scenario or task performance, to perform a comprehensive evaluation of the whole system rather than a task-specific evaluation.
[43/S3/F1] Available checklists for evaluating interfaces include detailed system-specific checklists and checklists focused on broader usability principles, and the broader checklists contain fewer heuristics because they are broader in nature and not specific to any particular interface.
[43/S3/F2] Nielsen's 10 heuristics is a general set of heuristics proposed in 1994 that is primarily related to graphical user interfaces (GUIs) but equally applicable to most interactive systems.
[43/S3/F3] Nielsen's 10 heuristics consist of: 1) visibility of system status, in which the status of the system should be visible to the user; 2) match between system and the real world, in which what the user does on the system should closely match the user's real-world experience; 3) user control and freedom, concerning whether the user is given freedom and what kind of controls are provided; 4) consistency and standards, in which elements and layout should be consistent across different screens and standards should be followed; 5) error prevention, in which the design should help the user prevent errors; 6) recognition rather than recall, in which the user should not have to remember what to do but should be able to recognize from elements what to do because recollection is more difficult than recognition; 7) flexibility and efficiency of use; 8) aesthetic and minimalist design, in which the design should have a minimum number of elements and should be aesthetically pleasing; 9) help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors; and 10) help and documentation, in which the system should provide help and documentation support.
[43/S3/F4] Nielsen's 10 heuristics can be used to identify usability problems at an early stage of design on the basis of simple low-fidelity prototypes, primarily horizontal prototypes, with vertical prototypes optional in heuristic evaluation, unlike the cognitive walkthrough method, where a vertical prototype is mandatory.
[43/S3/F5] Heuristic evaluation requires a team of at least three to five evaluators, who can be members of design teams or other skilled or expert designers not necessarily involved in the design team.
[43/S3/F6] In heuristic evaluation, each evaluator is given the prototype and the checklist of heuristics, evaluates the prototype against each heuristic, reports whether each heuristic is followed or violated and, if violated, gives reasons, and the evaluators' reports are combined and compiled to identify the violated heuristics and major issues.
