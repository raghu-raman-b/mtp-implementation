[44/S0/F0] User evaluation is a quick evaluation technique that obtains feedback by asking users directly, and it requires user involvement, unlike expert evaluation techniques in which user involvement was optional.
[44/S0/F1] User evaluation is often called evaluation with self-reports, and user feedback is collected with rating scales, with the data sometimes called subjective or preference data.
[44/S0/F2] In user evaluation, collecting user feedback with rating scales is easy compared with more rigorous evaluation techniques.
[44/S0/F3] In user evaluation, the items provided to users to rate are questions or statements, and these items are commonly called questionnaires although they need not contain questions.
[44/S0/F4] One major concern in user evaluation is that identifying suitable questionnaires is challenging.
[44/S0/F5] In user evaluation, data can be collected after every task and at the end of the session, collecting data at both points is desirable if feasible, otherwise one of the two points must be chosen, and suitable questionnaires must be created for the point at which data are collected, so the questionnaires used after every task need not be the same as those used after the whole session.
[44/S0/F6] Well-known questionnaires available for post-task data collection include the After Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ), designed by Lewis in 1991, and the Expectation Measure (EM), designed by Albert and Dixon in 2003.
[44/S0/F7] Well-known questionnaires available for post-session data collection include the System Usability Scale (SUS), designed by Brooke in 1996; the Computer System Usability Questionnaire (CSUQ), designed by Lewis in 1995; the Questionnaire for User Interface Satisfaction (QUIS), designed by Chin et al. in 1988; and the Usefulness Satisfaction and Ease of Use questionnaire (USE), proposed by Lund in 2001.
[44/S1/F0] The After Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ) is a post-task questionnaire consisting of three statements: 'I am satisfied with the ease of completing the tasks in this scenario'; 'I am satisfied with the amount of time it took to complete the tasks in this scenario'; and 'I am satisfied with the support information that is comprised of online help, messages, documentation when completing the tasks'.
[44/S1/F1] In the After Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ), users rate each statement on a seven-point rating scale in which rating 1 means strongly disagree with the statement and rating 7 means strongly agree with the statement.
[44/S1/F2] The three statements of the After Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ) can be roughly mapped to the three usability measures in the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) definition: ease of completing the tasks relates to effectiveness, the amount of time taken to complete the tasks relates to efficiency, and the three statements together represent satisfaction.
[44/S1/F3] Feedback from the After Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ) can be summarised by taking either the average rating for each statement across all users who participated in the evaluation or the average rating for all statements taken together.
[44/S2/F0] The System Usability Scale (SUS) is a post-session questionnaire, containing statements rather than questions, that is administered at the end of a session after users have completed all the tasks.
[44/S2/F1] The System Usability Scale (SUS) consists of 10 numbered statements: 1, 'I think that I would like to use this system frequently' (positive); 2, 'I found the system unnecessarily complex' (negative); 3, 'I thought the system was easy to use' (positive); 4, 'I think I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use the system' (negative); 5, 'I found the various functions in this system were well integrated' (positive); 6, 'I thought the system was too inconsistent' (negative); 7, 'I would imagine that most people would learn to use the system very quickly' (positive); 8, 'I found the system very cumbersome to use' (negative); 9, 'I felt very confident using the system' (positive); and 10, 'I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system' (negative); each even-numbered statement is the negatively worded counterpart of the preceding odd-numbered statement.
[44/S2/F2] Each System Usability Scale (SUS) statement is rated by the user on a 5-point agreement scale, where 1 indicates strongly disagree and 5 indicates strongly agree.
[44/S2/F3] The System Usability Scale (SUS) score S is calculated from all 10 statement ratings r_i, with i from 1 to 10, as S = 2.5 × (Σ_{i∈{1,3,5,7,9}}(r_i − 1) + Σ_{i∈{2,4,6,8,10}}(5 − r_i)), where r_i is the user's rating for statement i.
[44/S2/F4] The System Usability Scale (SUS) score is interpreted directly as a percentage, and a score of 70% or higher is typically considered desirable, indicating that the interface is usable or has only minimal problems, while a score below 70% may indicate usability problems that may require a design review.
[44/S2/F5] Unlike expert evaluation techniques, which give designers more detailed reports from each evaluator, the System Usability Scale (SUS) gives a quick general indication from user ratings of whether usability problems exist but does not explain the reasons, so designers must identify the specific problems through further brainstorming or by examining the scores of individual statements.
[44/S3/F0] The questionnaires identified as ASQ and SUS are generic questionnaires applicable to any interactive system, not specifically meant for extended reality (XR) systems.
[44/S3/F1] The simulator sickness questionnaire (SSQ) is the most extensively used of the questionnaires specifically designed for cyber sickness in extended reality (XR).
[44/S3/F2] The virtual reality sickness questionnaire (VRSQ) and the cyber sickness questionnaire (CSQ) are questionnaires relevant to cyber sickness in extended reality (XR).
[44/S3/F3] The cyber sickness questionnaire (CSQ) is good for evaluating virtual reality (VR) experiences with head-mounted displays (HMDs).
[44/S3/F4] Shorter, more precise, and more concise questionnaires for extended reality (XR) that assess specific conditions include the misery scale (MIS) and the fast motion sickness scale (FMS).
[44/S3/F5] Susceptibility to cyber sickness (CS) in extended reality (XR) can be measured using the Motion History Questionnaire (MHQ), the Motion Sickness Susceptibility Questionnaire (MSSQ), and the Visually Induced Motion Sickness Susceptibility Questionnaire (VIMSSQ).
[44/S3/F6] Questionnaires commonly used to assess overall discomfort in immersive extended reality (XR) experiences include the nausea scale and the motion sickness assessment questionnaire (MSAQ).
[44/S4/F0] The Majuli VR Tour was developed with Unity engine version 2021.3.22f1 for the Oculus Quest 2 platform and Android smartphones, with Android 6.0 as the operating-system requirement for both versions.
[44/S4/F1] The Majuli VR Tour used the Extended Reality (XR) toolkit for its Oculus Quest version and the Google Virtual Reality (VR) software development kit (SDK) for its Google Cardboard version.
[44/S4/F2] Autodesk Maya software was used to create the three-dimensional (3D) models for the Majuli VR Tour immersive experience, using real images as references and as textures on the modeled objects.
[44/S4/F3] The initial Majuli VR Tour application size was 9.85 gigabytes (GB) because its content was heavy to cover the entire geographic spread.
[44/S4/F4] To reduce the space requirements of the Majuli VR Tour, all videos were stored in cloud services and loaded from the cloud when a user clicked a video thumbnail in the Oculus Quest video player, so the videos were not always stored on the client device, reducing the application size to 5.1 gigabytes (GB).
[44/S4/F5] Because the Majuli VR Tour application was 5.1 gigabytes (GB) and not suitable for mobile deployment, a mobile version for Google Cardboard was made by compressing high-resolution 360-degree and two-dimensional (2D) images and removing most video playback support, reducing the application size to 0.7 gigabytes (GB).
[44/S4/F6] The Oculus Quest version of the Majuli VR Tour used 597 images, 11 cultural videos, 8 drone videos, and 5 360-degree videos, while the Google Cardboard mobile version used all 597 images in compressed form, no cultural videos, no drone videos, and 1 360-degree video for the initial experience.

[44/S5/F6] At different points in the 360-degree-image Majuli virtual reality (VR) Tour implementation, cultural videos and activities are available, and a notification for cultural videos is played on the tablet interface.

[44/S5/F11] The three-dimensional (3D) models in the model-based Majuli virtual reality (VR) Tour implementation use textures from real images to create a realistic-looking appearance.

[44/S5/F13] The mobile-device Majuli virtual reality (VR) Tour implementation is a reduced form of the full Oculus Quest experience, with simpler controls and fewer features, but its navigation, selection, and manipulation modes are more or less the same.
[44/S5/F14] To optimize size in the mobile-device Majuli virtual reality (VR) Tour implementation, most videos and cultural videos are omitted, and only some prominent videos are used.
