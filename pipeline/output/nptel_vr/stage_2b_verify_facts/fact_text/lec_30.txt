[30/S0/F0] Sound perception occurs at three levels—social, warning, and primitive—which together give a person an overall idea of the surroundings.
[30/S0/F1] Social-level sound perception is the level of sound perception that helps an individual carry out social interactions with other individuals.
[30/S0/F2] Warning-level sound perception is the level of sound perception that provides an alert or warning about something, such as an event.
[30/S0/F3] Primitive-level sound perception is the intuitive perception of naive sounds such as background noises that people mostly ignore, and it may not lead to any meaningful action by the individual.
[30/S0/F4] Identifying the desired sound perception level helps a developer generate suitable auditory effects when generating sound effects for a virtual reality (VR) system; for example, if social-level perception is required, the developer should generate stimuli that support social-level perception rather than stimuli that typically produce primitive-level perception.
[30/S1/F0] Psychophysics is the scientific study of perceptual phenomena that are produced by physical stimuli.
[30/S1/F1] Psychoacoustics is a more specific area of psychophysics and is the scientific study of perceptual phenomena that are produced by acoustic stimuli or sound.
[30/S1/F2] Stevens' power law is a result from psychophysics that characterizes the relationship between the magnitude of a physical stimulus and its perceived magnitude by the equation P = C M^x, where M is the magnitude or intensity of the original stimulus, P is the perceived magnitude, x is the exponent that relates actual magnitude to perceived magnitude, and C is a constant that depends on units.
[30/S1/F3] The just noticeable difference (JND) is a result from psychophysics that specifies the amount of change in a stimulus that is required for subjects to perceive it as changed in at least 50 percent of trials.
[30/S1/F4] Weber's law captures the just noticeable difference (JND), the change in magnitude that is noticeable, by representing it as delta M divided by M equals C, where M is the magnitude or intensity of the stimulus, delta M is the just noticeable difference, and C is a constant; equivalently, delta M equals M times C.
[30/S1/F5] Weber's law and Stevens' power law are laws from psychophysics that also apply to psychoacoustics, or auditory perception, when physical stimuli are replaced by acoustic stimuli.
[30/S1/F6] For the perceived loudness of a 3000 hertz (Hz) pure tone, the Stevens' power law exponent x is 0.67, so a much higher pressure level is perceived as only a bit louder because the exponent is less than 1.
[30/S1/F7] For a change in a stimulus to be perceived, it must be above the just noticeable difference (JND); for it not to be perceived, it must be within or below the JND.
[30/S2/F0] In pitch perception, the frequency of a sound wave is referred to as pitch.
[30/S2/F1] For sound frequency, the just noticeable difference (JND) increases as frequency increases: for frequencies less than 1000 Hz, humans can detect a frequency change of less than 1 Hz; at 10,000 Hz the JND is about 100 Hz; in percentage terms, at low frequencies humans can detect frequency differences of 0.1% or less, whereas at higher frequencies a difference of at least 1% is required.
[30/S3/F0] Sound localization is a main area of psychoacoustics that estimates the location of a sound source by hearing it.
[30/S3/F1] Sound localization is crucial in virtual reality (VR) because it helps the user perceive the orientation and position of objects relative to the user by matching auditory and visual cues; a mismatch can break presence and cause cybersickness.
[30/S3/F2] The minimum audible angle (MAA) is the minimum angular variation that can be detected by a human listener, obtained by applying the just noticeable difference (JND) concept to sound localization and represented in a spherical coordinate system with the listener's head as the origin.
[30/S3/F3] The spherical coordinate system used to represent a sound source location has three parameters: azimuth, the angle in the horizontal plane between the forward direction and the projection of the sound source direction, ranging from -180 degrees to 180 degrees; elevation, the angle between the actual source direction and the horizontal plane, ranging from -90 degrees to 90 degrees; and radius, the distance from the listener's head origin to the source.
[30/S3/F4] The minimum audible angle (MAA) depends on both the frequency and direction of the sound source: at some frequencies and locations it can be as low as 1 degree, while at other combinations localization can be very difficult and the MAA can be extremely high.
[30/S4/F0] Sound localization cues are either monaural, involving a single ear, or binaural, involving both ears.
[30/S4/F1] Interaural level difference (ILD) is a binaural sound localization cue that is the difference in sound magnitude heard by each ear, with the ear without obstruction receiving a stronger sound, and the difference can indicate the direction from which the sound is coming.
[30/S4/F2] An acoustic shadow occurs when a sound wave does not reach one ear, caused by an object in front of the sound source or by the position of the head.
[30/S4/F3] Interaural time difference is a binaural sound localization cue from different sound arrival times at the ears; in an average human head, the ears are 21.5 centimeters apart, sound travels that distance in about 0.6 milliseconds, and this small time difference can be used to locate the source.
[30/S4/F4] The auditory component of an extended reality (XR) system should ideally be non-stationary, moving with the user, and interactive, dependent on the listener's actions.
[30/S4/F5] Head-related transfer functions (HRTF) model how a listener receives sound from a location in space, and this model is relevant for dealing with sound in any system, including extended reality (XR) systems.
[30/S4/F6] Head-related transfer functions (HRTF) depend on the size and shape of the head, ears, and ear canal, and every person has a unique HRTF.
[30/S4/F7] Because every person has a unique head-related transfer function (HRTF), it is impractical to determine individual users' HRTFs when building a system, so an HRTF for an average listener is developed and used.
[30/S4/F8] A pair of head-related transfer functions (HRTF) for both ears can be used to synthesize binaural sound, and by generating sound effects or placing sound receivers according to the HRTF, a localization effect can be implemented in an extended reality (XR) system.
