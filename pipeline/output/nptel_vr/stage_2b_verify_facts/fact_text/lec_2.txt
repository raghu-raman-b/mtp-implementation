[2/S0/F0] Although adoption statistics can be misleading, earlier use forecasts discussed in the course indicate that extended reality, which covers augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR), has commercial potential in video games (about 34% or more than one third of applications), healthcare, engineering, live events, video entertainment, real estate, retail, and military (about 4% of application areas, a figure that may change); because worldwide military budgets are huge, even 4% is a large monetary amount, so penetration in the commercial space can be expected both percentage-wise and value-wise.
[2/S0/F1] The major current application domains for virtual reality (VR) discussed are gaming and entertainment, healthcare, education, the automobile industry, travel and tourism (including virtual tourism and digital heritage preservation), real estate and architecture, and defense.
[2/S0/F2] Gaming and entertainment is an early and current virtual reality (VR) application domain; recent immersive gaming products use immersive technologies to make gaming more acceptable and popular, adding value to the gaming industry, and players are expected to wear headsets or other VR devices to experience immersion.
[2/S0/F3] Healthcare is a prominent virtual reality (VR) application domain, primarily for medical education and training, and mixed reality (MR) is also used; in it, potential doctors can learn about body organs, how they work, how they look, and their different parts, and immersive simulations help when obtaining cadavers is difficult.
[2/S0/F4] Education is an emerging application domain for immersive technologies from primary to tertiary levels; appropriate exposure to immersive technologies can improve learners' engagement and learning outcomes compared with standard teaching and learning methods, although excessive duration may create problems; examples include classroom education at primary or secondary level and higher-education institutes developing immersive topic-specific teaching and learning solutions.
[2/S0/F5] Although it is not yet very popular, the automobile industry is an evolving virtual reality (VR) application area in which companies create immersive experiences of newly launched car models without showroom visits or actual driving to increase customer satisfaction and experience; although primarily intended for potential buyers, it can also be used in automobile design and development.
[2/S0/F6] Travel and tourism is an emerging application area for immersive technologies, including virtual tours and virtual tourism, which let users visit faraway places without physically going there and save money and physical effort.
[2/S0/F7] Digital heritage preservation and heritage tourism are emerging immersive-technology applications for heritage places that cannot be physically visited by large numbers of users or in all parts for preservation reasons; they let users explore each part virtually, preserve the space digitally despite physical decay, and increase the space's lifespan and potential to be visited by more users.
[2/S0/F8] Real estate and architecture is a prominent virtual reality (VR) application area in which potential buyers can explore upcoming properties, including properties that do not yet exist, without visiting the place, enabling them to make informed purchase decisions before spending large amounts on real estate.
[2/S0/F9] Defense is a major virtual reality (VR) application area for training, including specialized military training that simulates vehicles, soldiers, and combat environments with constraints; virtual boot camp and combat training using head-mounted displays (HMDs) with motion trackers, full-body tracking systems, and wireless weapon controllers; training for military medical personnel; and maintenance and assembly technician training, with applications catering to these needs already existing.
[2/S1/F0] Immersion in virtual reality (VR) means that the user's senses are blocked so that the user receives no input from the real-world environment and receives input only from the virtual world.
[2/S1/F1] From a systems point of view, virtual reality (VR) systems are broadly categorized into non-immersive, semi-immersive, and fully immersive systems because market applications offer varying degrees of immersion rather than all being fully immersive.
[2/S1/F2] A non-immersive virtual reality (VR) system is a specific, limited class of computer application, not any computer application, that provides a computer-generated environment in which the user can control characters or activities while retaining control of the physical environment, and the virtual environment does not directly interact with the user.
[2/S1/F3] A non-immersive virtual reality (VR) application is a regular computer system or software that completely takes away the user's attention, so the user is psychologically engaged and may not experience the real world, although the user's eyes, ears, and other sensory organs remain open and the user is physically attached to the real world.
[2/S1/F4] Non-immersive virtual reality (VR) includes specifically gaming applications, such as games on PlayStation, Xbox, or similar systems and games like World of Warcraft or similar games, that rely on computer or video game console displays and input devices such as keyboards, mice, and handheld controllers.
[2/S1/F5] Semi-immersive virtual reality (VR) is in between non-immersive and fully immersive VR, giving the user a partial immersive experience by blocking some but not all senses; for example, the eyes may be blocked while ears and skin remain exposed to real-world signals.
[2/S1/F6] Semi-immersive virtual reality (VR) can be a three-dimensional virtual environment in which users can move by themselves through a computer screen, a VR box, or a VR headset, with a VR headset being the common example; users typically have no real physical movements, and examples include virtual tours using only a headset, which can be device-based or web-based.
[2/S1/F7] Semi-immersive virtual reality (VR) systems are the most cost-effective and commonly used form of VR, and most commercial immersive applications are semi-immersive because they give partial immersion while leaving the user free to sense the real world.
[2/S1/F8] A fully immersive virtual reality (VR) system completely blocks the user's ability to sense the real world by blocking all senses, so the user senses only the things happening in the virtual world, feels physically present in it, and has the user's sense of reality replaced by virtual realism.
[2/S1/F9] Fully immersive virtual reality (VR) systems, such as dedicated virtual shooting game zones, use many devices such as helmets, gloves, body connectors with sense detectors, and body suits, so users view the virtual environment through a helmet or head-mounted display and their actual movements, such as running, jumping, crouching, shooting, and throwing, their reactions, and even a blink of an eye are detected and translated into actions in the virtual world while the user cannot sense anything in the real world.
[2/S1/F10] Among virtual reality (VR) systems, fully immersive systems are the costliest and commercially rare because they are difficult to afford, while semi-immersive and non-immersive systems are more common.
