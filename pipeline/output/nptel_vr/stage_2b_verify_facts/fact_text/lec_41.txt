[41/S0/F0] The design of the Majuli virtual reality (VR) tour was framed by its broader objectives of capturing tangible and intangible heritage, enhancing user presence, and minimizing cyber sickness, with corresponding design considerations of user experience design, exploration of the island, and handling cyber sickness.
[41/S0/F1] A large, content-rich geographic spread in the Majuli virtual reality (VR) tour can lead to cyber sickness if the tour is not carefully designed, because exploring that spread requires time.
[41/S0/F2] The final design of the Majuli virtual reality (VR) tour was developed using the interactive system development lifecycle (ISDL) model, an iterative design-prototype-evaluate cycle that started with a design based on design guidelines and continuously refined design ideas from feedback and observations obtained by involving end users passively or actively.
[41/S0/F3] Initial prototypes for the Majuli virtual reality (VR) tour were influenced by observations of the behaviors and preferences of the island's visitors, who were the potential users, and those observations informed design choices about which locations to cover, how to categorize and identify user groups, and how to integrate cultural videos and navigation features suitable for those user groups.
[41/S0/F4] User feedback and interaction with users for the Majuli virtual reality (VR) tour led to incorporated design elements such as a minimap, a notification system, and user controls.
[41/S1/F0] In user experience design, the first stage is to identify the users, so that the experience can be designed accordingly.
[41/S1/F1] The three distinct user categories identified for the virtual tour of the island are pilgrims, students and tourists, each with its own preferences for visiting or exploring the island, and the virtual tour's content and features were tailored to those preferences, which were collected through contextual inquiry methods.
[41/S1/F2] The information points on the island were identified based on, among other things, interaction with users, field trips, user feedback, and the need to cover the entire island.
[41/S1/F3] The 24 information points on the island are 9 prominent satras, 6 villages, and 9 towns and other places of importance.
[41/S1/F4] The virtual tour of the island was designed to cover all 24 information points on the island so that it could comprehensively capture the tangible and intangible heritage aspects of the entire island.
[41/S2/F0] The starting point of the virtual tour uses a curved screen in an immersive setting to show an introductory video about Majuli island and a tutorial video that acquaints users with how to use the system.
[41/S2/F1] A boat ride experience at the very start of the virtual tour uses a 360-degree boat ride video to give the user the feeling of being transported by boat from one bank of a river to the other bank, that is, from the mainland to the island or from the island to the mainland.
[41/S2/F2] Before the actual virtual tour experience begins, the user chooses a user group, indicated by icons for the user categories pilgrims, students, or tourists.
[41/S3/F0] Preprocessing of the collected images and videos to improve the visual appearance and the user's quality of experience involved using high-resolution content and removing unwanted elements, such as the tripod of a 360-degree camera or its shadow.
[41/S3/F1] The images and videos, mostly the images, were edited with image editing tools for uniform illumination because the 360-degree images captured at different times have different illumination and changing illumination while the user moves from one image to another will make the user feel that something is odd.
[41/S3/F2] Ambient sounds such as temple bells, birds chirping, and bike horns were added and played depending on the place being visited to enhance realism and create an authentic ambience, with temple bells played for a satra or a religious site.
[41/S4/F0] The virtual tablet interface provided to users acts as the main interface and provides navigation tools, a video player, and access to information content.
[41/S4/F1] The navigation tools of the virtual tablet interface include a left-side island map presented as a world in miniature and buttons for opening the mini map.
[41/S4/F2] The video player in the virtual tablet interface has a volume controller and other controls and is used to view cultural videos or top-view drone videos of a place.
[41/S4/F3] The virtual tablet interface provides access to information content through info elements.
[41/S4/F4] The design of the virtual tablet interface is inspired by real-world tablet use so that it is familiar and easy for users to use.
[41/S5/F0] A minimap is a world-in-miniature map for a specific place on the island, such as a village or town; it differs in appearance from the global island map and is provided at each of the island's 24 information points to give the user a sense of belonging or local orientation to that place.
[41/S5/F1] Each place on the island, such as a village, town, or satra, is modeled using captured 360-degree images, with the number of images per place varying, for example more than five or six, 10, or 50.
[41/S5/F2] On the minimap, white dots represent the arrangement of the 360-degree images; the user navigates by moving from one image to another along the dots, and the arrangement provides direction and orientation; the user's current image is highlighted green.
[41/S5/F3] An arrow shown to the user indicates a direction, and selecting the arrow moves the user in that direction along the arrangement of the 360-degree images.
[41/S5/F4] At each place, notifications alert users to available cultural videos; when there is something to see at a spot, the information icon becomes active, and selecting it shows whether a cultural video is available at that spot, which can be played on the tablet interface.
[41/S5/F5] For location awareness, the system uses dual maps: a global map, which is a world-in-miniature map of the entire island, on the tablet interface and a local minimap on the local interface, to reduce clutter and provide clear navigation support.
[41/S5/F6] The global map and the local minimap support wayfinding and orientation awareness by showing the entire world in miniature; the minimap provides dynamic spatial orientation awareness about which direction to move, helping users create a mental map and wayfind.
[41/S6/F0] In the island tour, each place is designed using a series of 360-degree images, so moving from one point to another is moving from one image to another.
[41/S6/F1] View interpolation ensures a smooth visual transition between two 360-degree images by blurring their boundaries, preventing the sudden movement or jerks that an unhandled image transition would cause and that reduce the user experience.
[41/S6/F2] Navigation arrows guide the user from one image to another, prevent random movement, and create a seamless exploration experience while supporting smooth travel.
[41/S6/F3] A virtual vehicle metaphor is a three-dimensional (3D) model of a vehicle that facilitates long-distance travel within the tour.
[41/S6/F4] Walking-based navigation is used for a single spot, but exploring the entire island by walking-based navigation would take a lot of time and may lead to cybersickness, so a different travel technique was required.
[41/S6/F5] The virtual vehicle travel technique gives the user a seated viewpoint inside the vehicle when it is selected, changes the surrounding scenes as the vehicle moves to create the feeling of motion, and lets the user operate the vehicle through ray casting interactions to provide a direction of movement and select a destination, to which the vehicle moves.
[41/S6/F6] The virtual vehicle travel technique is a selection-based locomotion technique in which the route is pre-planned and the user selects a destination to select a route; it is route selection, not target selection or instantaneous movement.
[41/S7/F0] In the virtual tour system, the tablet interface includes a video player that allows users to access cultural and ecological videos relevant to the user's current location in the virtual world; the videos can be 360-degree videos, drone videos, or regular two-dimensional (2D) videos.
[41/S7/F1] The virtual tour system's tablet interface shows a list of videos available for a given spot, and when the user is at a spot the corresponding material for that spot is added to the interface so the user can choose it, giving access to the most relevant information for that spot, and that relevant information is also determined by the user type.
[41/S7/F2] The virtual tour system provides textual descriptions of a place as informative notifications and descriptions that provide additional context about locations and significant spots.
[41/S7/F3] In the virtual tour system, point-of-interest awareness is supported by directional arrows that indicate which way to move and guide users toward points of interest, and by information icons that, when selected, provide further details about the place, mostly as textual information, so users do not overlook important elements in the environment.
[41/S8/F0] The system design implemented a controlled navigation technique to minimize cybersickness, in which users navigate or teleport using a joystick, so that controlled movement does not support abrupt movements that can cause discomfort and reduces the chances of discomfort.
[41/S8/F1] In vehicular movement, the speed of change of viewpoint may cause cybersickness if it is not chosen carefully.
[41/S8/F2] Consistent visual cues and smooth transitions in the system design help maintain spatial orientation, give the user a better feeling and awareness of the place, reduce anxiety and sensory conflicts, and in the long term help avoid severe cybersickness effects.
[41/S9/F0] In the design of immersive experiences, the term design refers to interface and interaction design rather than code design.
