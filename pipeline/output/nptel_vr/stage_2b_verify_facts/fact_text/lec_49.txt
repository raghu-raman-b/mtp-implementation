[49/S0/F0] Optimization in virtual reality (VR) can be considered from two perspectives: optimized experience, that is, psychophysiological experience optimization, and optimized resource utilization or resource requirement, that is, technological optimization; these requirements are present in any extended reality (XR) system, but they gain more prominence in an immersive experience.
[49/S0/F1] In virtual reality (VR), experience optimization can roughly be taken to mean designing the experience to minimize cyber sickness or the possibility of its induction.
[49/S0/F2] Extended reality (XR) systems, and virtual reality (VR) systems in particular, are generally resource-hungry; if their resource requirements are too high, the systems will not be affordable and it will be difficult to make products for the masses, where affordability is a major concern.
[49/S0/F3] In virtual reality (VR), effective resource optimization means minimizing resource requirements while maximizing the immersive experience, without compromising it and, to the extent possible, without reducing its quality; this requires balancing the trade-offs between resource requirements and experience.
[49/S1/F0] Cybersickness, also known as visually induced motion sickness (VIMS), is a condition that can affect individuals when they interact with virtual reality (VR), and it is not a single-factor effect because a variety of factors can affect it.
[49/S1/F1] The factors that can induce cybersickness are broadly divided into internal factors, which include the age of the user, the gender of the user, the user's susceptibility to motion sickness, the physical condition of the user, and the emotional condition of the user, and external factors, which include hardware factors such as display resolution, latency, latency jitter, type of display, and type of input, and virtual reality (VR) content factors such as visual motion, means of locomotion, field of view, type of content, duration, and visual complexity.
[49/S1/F2] Finding feasible solutions to reduce the impact of cybersickness while a user is experiencing virtual reality (VR) is important because it is supposed to greatly enhance overall user experience and make VR more appealing to a wider range of people.
[49/S1/F3] Cybersickness mitigation strategies often come at the cost of presence because reducing cybersickness may make the user feel more in an artificial environment than in a real environment and less likely to obtain a proper immersive experience.
[49/S1/F4] The challenge in developing cybersickness mitigation strategies is to avoid compromising presence, which is indirectly related to user experience.
[49/S2/F0] Cybersickness is minimized by reducing either the duration of exposure to the virtual world or the distance covered during navigation in the virtual environment, while presence is preserved or maximized.
[49/S2/F1] Virtual locomotion techniques (VLTs), used to navigate or perform a virtual reality (VR) tour by moving from one point to another, are broadly categorized into walking-based techniques (full gait, partial gait, and gait negation techniques, involving physical walking), steering-based techniques, selection-based techniques, and manipulation-based techniques.
[49/S2/F2] Virtual reality (VR) tours are categorized into user-controlled tours, in which the user controls how and where to navigate using controllers or other input modalities, and system-controlled tours, in which the system or application controls the user's movement, often following a predetermined path or sequence of movements.
[49/S2/F3] System-controlled virtual reality (VR) tours are often an effortless experience with reduced cognitive demands because the system decides how and where the user moves, allowing the user to concentrate more on the experience itself.
[49/S2/F4] Lack of user involvement in system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tours makes them less realistic and defeats the purpose of presence because the user is unable to control the movement.
[49/S3/F0] In a virtual reality (VR) tour, a system-controlled tour is a navigation mode in which the system forces the user to move from one place to another.
[49/S3/F1] A realistic walking experience in a system-controlled tour is important because it helps the user feel present rather than controlled by the system and reduces awareness of the difference between realistic and unrealistic elements.
[49/S3/F2] System-controlled tours, also described as system-automated tours, typically do not allow continuous user input, making it difficult to align user input with walking speed or to judge whether the speed provided by the system is natural.
[49/S3/F3] Optic flow is the speed at which the visual scene changes, and movement is perceived through this change in the view of the surroundings; in a virtual reality (VR) tour, user movement appears as a constantly changing scene.
[49/S3/F4] When the navigation in a system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tour is assumed to be walking, the scene-change speed (optic flow) should match the user's actual, or typical, real-world walking speed rather than VR walking speed; otherwise the movement looks unrealistic because it may be too fast or too slow.
[49/S3/F5] A more realistic system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tour requires matching its optic flow to the user's instantaneous walking speed using a model of the user's typical walking behavior, because a constant optic flow is not very realistic as people rarely walk at constant speed.
