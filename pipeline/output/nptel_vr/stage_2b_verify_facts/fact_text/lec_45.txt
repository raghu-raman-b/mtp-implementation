[45/S0/F0] Empirical evaluation, also called empirical study, usability study, or empirical research, is a stage in interactive system development in which the usability of a developed system is evaluated with end users.
[45/S0/F1] In interactive system development, empirical evaluation follows the code testing stage, which produces an executable system but does not establish that the system is usable.
[45/S1/F0] Quick prototype evaluations are limited-scale early evaluations that use a limited number of evaluators and evaluate prototypes, so they can quickly identify some major usability issues but cannot give a conclusive result about the overall usability of the end system.
[45/S1/F1] A conclusive usability assessment of the overall system requires evaluating the whole fully implemented system rather than a prototype, preferably with a larger number of users, through a controlled experiment that collects and analyzes user behavioral data.
[45/S1/F2] A controlled experiment used for usability evaluation is generally referred to as empirical research, an empirical study, or a user study, and in the context of usability evaluation it can also be called a usability study.
[45/S2/F0] An empirical study broadly has four stages: formulating the research questions, determining the variables after the research questions (without which a controlled experiment cannot be designed and executed), designing and executing the experiment to collect data, and properly, scientifically, and objectively analyzing the data to reach a reliable conclusion about the system's overall usability aspects.
[45/S3/F0] An empirical study, after a system has been fully implemented, is the process of setting up an experiment, executing the experiment, and collecting empirical data for analysis to measure the usability of the implemented interface.
[45/S3/F1] The first stage in an empirical study is formulating a research question, and the experiment is conducted to find the answer to that question, with observations or empirical data collection and data analysis performed based on the question.
[45/S3/F2] The research question 'Is the new text entry graphical user interface (GUI) good?' is problematic because asking several users, for example five users, to judge its quality can produce divided judgments, such as two good, two poor or very bad, and one in between, neither good nor bad, so no answer to the question is likely and no conclusion can be drawn.
[45/S4/F0] Research question 2 (RQ2), asking whether a proposed text input interface is better than Microsoft Word (MS Word), elicits somewhat more concrete feedback than standalone evaluation, but it does not give a definitive quality conclusion because 'better' means more or fewer features to different users.
[45/S4/F1] Research question 3 (RQ3), asking whether the new interface lets a user enter text faster than Microsoft Word (MS Word), is less prone to subjective interpretation than a vague quality question because it asks users to complete a text input task and compare the two interfaces on speed.
[45/S4/F2] Dependence on subjective feedback is problematic and may not support a definitive conclusion, even for a clear question about which text entry interface is faster, because responses can split.
[45/S4/F3] An experiment collects objective data for a text entry speed question by having all users input the same text, logging their speed as characters per minute (CPM), and comparing CPM values to objectively determine which interface is faster.
[45/S5/F0] A testable research question, also called a hypothesis, is a question for which a study can be conducted to collect objective data, such as text entry speed, and draw objective conclusions independently of users' subjective interpretation.
[45/S5/F1] A non-testable research question, also called an untestable question, is a question that cannot be directly tested objectively because it depends on users' subjective interpretation and feedback, so a controlled experiment cannot be set up to collect objective data and, generally, a reliable scientific conclusion cannot be drawn.
[45/S5/F2] Research questions should not be framed from generalized concepts such as whether something is good, because such concepts are ambiguous and can lead to untestable questions.
[45/S5/F3] Testable research questions should be used instead of untestable questions, because user feedback on untestable questions is subjectively interpreted and generally cannot support a reliable, definite scientific conclusion, whereas testable questions allow reliable, definite conclusions.
[45/S5/F4] A more specific testable research question, such as whether one interface is faster than another, may answer a specific aspect but may not answer the broader generalized question of whether one interface is better, because faster and better are different concepts.
[45/S5/F5] To balance the trade-off between testability and generalization, stage 1 of an empirical study frames multiple testable research questions covering different aspects of a system and combines their conclusions to reach a definitive conclusion about a broader untestable generalized question, such as the overall goodness of an interface.
[45/S6/F0] A variable whose values are assigned and controlled is called an independent variable, also called a factor.
[45/S6/F1] The values assigned to an independent variable or factor are called the levels of that independent variable or factor.
[45/S6/F2] Factors combined with their levels give the test conditions for an experiment and are used to design the experiment; in the text-entry example, the interface factor has two levels, the course's design and MS Word, and these two levels give two test conditions in which text entry speed is observed with each.
[45/S6/F3] A dependent variable is a variable whose value depends on an independent variable or factor, specifically on its levels; in research question 3 (RQ3), the text entry rate (TER) is the dependent variable because its value depends on the interface, specifically the interface levels.
[45/S7/F0] Empirical evaluation, also called empirical study, empirical research, or usability research, means observation in the real world of real users.
[45/S7/F1] Empirical evaluation is carried out at the end, when the system is fully implemented, to obtain an idea of the overall usability of the system.
[45/S7/F2] The empirical research process has four stages, the first stage being research question framing and the second stage being variables.
[45/S7/F3] In empirical research, research question framing addresses broader generalizable questions that are untestable by framing multiple testable research questions, collecting objective data for each, reaching a definite conclusion for each, and combining those conclusions to draw a conclusion about the untestable questions.
[45/S7/F4] In the variables stage of empirical research, the variables in the testable questions are identified to design an experiment; the two most important variable types are independent variables, or factors, which can take levels or values, and dependent variables, which depend on the factors and levels.
