[26/S0/F0] The perceptual process proceeds from sensing, which is the lowest level, to perception, which is at a very abstract level, and perception can occur only after sense organs are stimulated by a particular type of stimulus and convert that input stimulus into neural impulses.
[26/S0/F1] The human brain contains around 86 billion neurons, and roughly 20 billion of its neurons are devoted to the cerebral cortex, the part of the brain that is responsible for perception and other functions such as attention, memory, language, and consciousness.
[26/S0/F2] The cerebral cortex is a large, heavily folded sheet of neurons about 3 millimeters thick, folded to fit into the skull.
[26/S1/F0] Perception and overall cognitive ability depend on the interconnection of neurons.
[26/S1/F1] A neuron has a nucleus or cell body, dendrites, an axon, branches of the axon, and axon terminals.
[26/S1/F2] The interconnection of neurons can be thought of as a directed graph or network, the original idea behind neural networks used in machine learning or deep learning, in which the nucleus or cell body is a node that processes input, the dendrites are input edges carrying neural impulses toward the cell body, and the axons are output edges carrying neural impulses away from the cell body.
[26/S1/F3] A neuron can aggregate information from other neurons, including information that has already been aggregated by other neurons, through the network of dendrites, and the processing that takes place in the nucleus or cell body is transmitted to one or more other neurons through axons.
[26/S1/F4] For an interconnection between neurons, an axon of one neuron is paired with a dendrite of the next neuron, but the axon and dendrite are not physically connected; communication occurs across a gap called the synapse, through which electrical or chemical signals are passed.
[26/S1/F5] In the human brain, each neuron has on average about 7,000 synaptic connections to other neurons, which results in about 10^15 edges in the brain's neuron-interconnection graph.
[26/S2/F0] Hierarchical processing is the neural processing performed as signals from sense organ receptors propagate through the neural network to the cerebral cortex.
[26/S2/F1] Hierarchical processing works by staged aggregation, in which signals from numerous receptors of different types are combined at successive levels by neurons until the cerebral cortex is reached, and aggregated information at each level is higher-level information rather than the same lower-level information.
[26/S2/F2] Hierarchical processing allows increasingly complex patterns to be detected in the stimulus.
[26/S3/F0] In perception, once sensory signals reach the cerebral cortex, the interpretation of a stimulus is produced by combining real-time sensory input with life experience or knowledge stored in long-term memory, not by sensory input alone, and this combination produces perceptual phenomena such as face recognition and song identification.
[26/S3/F1] The interpretation of a stimulus depends on the interpreter's long-term experience, so a person with less experience such as a newborn and a person with long experience such as an aged person can interpret a stimulus very differently.
[26/S3/F2] Overall perceptual processing is an outcome of hierarchical processing and the combination of real-time sensory input with long-term knowledge or experience.
[26/S4/F0] Proprioception is another aspect of perception, slightly different from direct perception through sensory organs such as vision, auditory sense, touch, smell, and taste; it is the ability to sense the relative positions of body parts and the amount of muscular effort involved in moving them, and because it has no direct sensory organ or receptor it can be called a sixth sense.
[26/S4/F1] The motor cortex, which controls body motion, sends signals called efference copies to other parts of the brain, including the cerebral cortex, to communicate what motions have been executed, and this information is processed to understand the relative positions of body parts and the amount of muscular effort required to move them.
[26/S5/F0] The brain processes perception by combining signals from multiple senses and proprioception with experiences learned over a long period and interpreting the inputs in coherent, consistent, and familiar learned ways.
[26/S5/F1] In an immersive virtual reality (VR) system, interfering with the brain's long-term learned sensory processing can create a mismatch because the brain cannot be retuned to process the new inputs in a short span of time.
[26/S5/F2] A sensory mismatch in a virtual reality (VR) system may be insignificant if the user's brain can adapt, but for other users it can cause cybersickness, with symptoms such as fatigue, headache, dizziness, and nausea.
[26/S5/F3] A mismatch between the brain's sensory inputs and its familiar processing can compromise presence by making the user consciously aware of the conflict and immediately understand that the experience is artificial rather than natural or real.
[26/S5/F4] Vection is the illusion of self-motion in which a person moving in a virtual environment while physically stationary receives visual input reporting movement or acceleration while the balance sense or proprioception reports no motion, creating a sensory conflict.
[26/S5/F5] Extended reality design should carefully balance the experiences presented by the various senses, giving an immersive experience by fooling the brain so that it processes the information in the intended way.
