[9/S0/F0] The goal of any virtual reality (VR) system is to create a high degree of presence, meaning that people believe they are in the real world while they are actually in a virtual world, and the closer a VR system is to this goal, the better the immersive experience it provides.
[9/S0/F1] For some people, reality is the gold standard, meaning that what is perceived and sensed in the real world is reality, so for them the goal of a virtual reality (VR) system is to match real-world experience as closely as possible.
[9/S0/F2] For some people, the concept of reality in a virtual reality (VR) system should be something beyond or surpassing their lived day-to-day reality, not an exact mimicry of the real world, so for them the goal of a VR system is to surpass their idea of reality.
[9/S0/F3] Different conceptions of reality make the evaluation of a virtual reality (VR) system ambiguous: a VR system that matches one group's sense of reality may not be considered good by another group, and a VR system that surpasses reality may seem creepy or difficult to comprehend to some.
[9/S1/F0] The uncanny valley effect is a finding from a scientific study of human perception of artificial objects, originally robots and extendable to digital representations, that digital representations of humans that appear almost real can evoke unease or eeriness rather than familiarity because of subtle imperfections that may not be readily recognizable at first.
[9/S1/F1] In the plot illustrating the uncanny valley effect, with human likeness on the horizontal axis (x-axis) and familiarity on the vertical axis (y-axis), as an artificial object or digital object in virtual reality (VR) becomes more human-like, familiarity first increases for both moving and stationary objects, then decreases, more sharply for moving objects, reaches a minimum in the uncanny valley region, typically between 70 and 85 percent human likeness, and then increases again.
[9/S1/F2] When creating a virtual reality (VR) experience, the uncanny valley effect means that trying to match reality exactly with human-like figures or realistic objects may not produce a good experience if their human-likeness falls in the uncanny valley, so there is a trade-off over how much realism to include.
[9/S2/F0] In virtual reality (VR), presence does not require photorealism or human-like objects; other cues may be equally useful for achieving higher presence.
[9/S2/F1] A virtual reality (VR) system developer or content creator should consider representational fidelity, interaction fidelity, and experiential fidelity, along with immersion and presence.
[9/S2/F2] Representational fidelity is the degree to which a virtual reality (VR) experience conveys, matches, or exploits familiarity with a place that is or could be on Earth.
[9/S2/F3] The highest representational fidelity is indicated by very good photorealistic immersive film content, such as 360-degree video or images of the real world in a virtual environment, while extremely low representational fidelity is indicated by purely abstract and non-objective worlds that are unfamiliar in real-world experience.
[9/S2/F4] Interaction fidelity is the degree to which the physical actions for a virtual task correspond to the physical actions for an equivalent real-world task, and it exploits real-world experience.
[9/S2/F5] High interaction fidelity involves intense realistic physical actions for virtual tasks, in which real-world physical activities are translated into virtual activities, while very low interaction fidelity involves no physical motion, such as sitting while moving forward in the virtual world by button press.
[9/S2/F6] Experiential fidelity is the degree to which the user's personal experience matches the intended experience of the virtual reality (VR) creator.
[9/S2/F7] Low experiential fidelity occurs when a free roaming virtual world with endless possibilities leads every usage to a different experience, because that is not what happens in the real world, while higher experiential fidelity results when real-world constraints are placed on virtual navigation, movement, and interaction, making the virtual experience closer to the real world.
[9/S2/F8] A virtual reality (VR) system developer or content creator should be aware of the uncanny valley effect and maintain representational, interaction, and experiential fidelities to the highest degree possible so that the uncanny valley effect does not affect the virtual world experience.
[9/S3/F0] The challenges of augmented reality (AR) systems are mostly technological rather than psychological because immersion is not a major concern and psychological effects are less important in augmented reality than in virtual reality (VR).
[9/S3/F1] The definition of augmented reality (AR) used to identify AR challenges was given by Azuma in 1997.
[9/S3/F2] Combining real and virtual content in augmented reality (AR) requires suitable display technology to overlay virtual content on the representation of the real world on the device, and the same display issue also exists in virtual reality (VR).
[9/S3/F3] Real-time interaction in augmented reality (AR) requires suitable forms of interaction, including different input devices and input modalities, so that the user can interact with digital content overlaid on a representation of the real world.
[9/S3/F4] Three-dimensional (3D) registration in augmented reality (AR) means anchoring virtual content in the real world at a suitable place so that it appears to be part of the physical environment and mixes seamlessly with real-world objects.
[9/S3/F5] To register virtual content in augmented reality (AR), the pose of the viewer relative to an anchor in the real world must be determined, where pose means position and orientation; without proper pose estimation, three-dimensional (3D) registration is difficult or impossible.
[9/S3/F6] Conceptually, pose determination in augmented reality (AR) has two parts, the registration phase and the tracking phase, which can be performed in one phase or as separate phases; the registration phase determines the viewer's pose relative to a real-world anchor when the user starts using the system, and the tracking phase updates that pose relative to a previously known pose because the user is likely to move in an AR application, so that virtual and real-world content appear properly mixed.
[9/S4/F0] The two major concerns in virtual reality (VR) systems are technological and psychological, and they lead to the major concepts immersion, which is tied to technological issues, and presence, which is tied to psychological issues.
[9/S4/F1] The uncanny valley effect in virtual reality (VR) is the phenomenon in which making environments as familiar as possible to the user's real-world experience can cause uneasiness or creepiness in the user's mind.
[9/S4/F2] To avoid the uncanny valley effect while building virtual reality (VR) systems, one should be aware of representational fidelity, experiential fidelity, and interaction fidelity.
[9/S4/F3] The major issues in augmented reality (AR) are mostly technological and consist of proper display technology, proper input mechanisms and technology, and pose estimation and updating, which is popularly called tracking, at the beginning and during system use.
