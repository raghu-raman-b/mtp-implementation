[50/S0/F0] A model of natural walking behavior can be used as a realistic walking behavior model for system-controlled virtual reality (VR) navigation to address the need for a realistic walking experience.
[50/S0/F1] The development of the realistic walking behavior model for system-controlled virtual reality (VR) navigation is carried out in four steps: empirical research to collect empirical data used to build the model, analysis of the data, creation of the model, and validation of the model.
[50/S1/F0] The empirical study was conducted to collect real-life data of a user moving from one point to another and to propose a speed estimation model.
[50/S1/F1] The empirical study involved 40 participants, comprising 26 male and 14 female participants, aged 19 to 42 years.
[50/S1/F2] In the empirical study, participant heights were recorded because earlier studies indicate that height affects walking behavior, and the participants' heights ranged from 150 to 184 centimeters.
[50/S1/F3] The corridor experiment used a corridor in a physical building in which seven paintings representing images of the seven wonders of the world were placed at distances of 20, 20, 13, 16, 6.4, 19, and 4 meters from the start.
[50/S1/F4] The seven paintings simulated points of interest in a system-controlled virtual tour, where a user may need to stop, pause, and then move.
[50/S1/F5] Participants were instructed to start walking from the starting place and stop at each painting to click a photograph, a task assigned to ensure that the user stopped after walking a predefined distance.
[50/S1/F6] Video recordings of the user's footsteps were made with a Samsung M30 smartphone camera fixed on the user's back and focused on the user's legs; the recording started just before the user started walking and was captured at 30 frames per second.
[50/S2/F0] In the data-analysis stage, participants' instantaneous step frequency (number of steps per second) was computed by manually playing the videos: a step was one foot strike to the next foot strike on the ground, foot-strike timestamps and distances between paintings were noted, and step frequencies at each step were calculated from the timestamps.
[50/S2/F1] When step frequency was plotted against distance, a pattern more or less followed by all participants was seen: step frequency started low, increased to a maximum, plateaued, and then decreased to zero at the target painting where the user stopped to click a photo; this pattern is called the slow-in-slow-out pattern.
[50/S2/F2] The velocity just before the target painting slowed because the user knew they had to stop there to click a photo.
[50/S3/F0] The slow-in-slow-out walking pattern observed in the study is similar to the Fourier-series approximation of a square pulse wave, which starts, remains approximately constant, and then returns to zero.
[50/S3/F1] A Fourier series with appropriate weights can approximate any continuous periodic function to arbitrary precision, and a practical approximation can be obtained by taking the first few terms of the corresponding Fourier expansion.
[50/S3/F2] The slow-in-slow-out walking pattern is modeled by a Fourier-series expansion of harmonically related sinusoids, and the weights of those sinusoids are calculated from empirical data.
[50/S3/F3] In virtual walking, the user's actual movement speed and perceived movement speed need not be the same, and generally an individual perceives visual or optic flow more slowly than the actual movement.
[50/S3/F4] A virtual tour should not change the scene at the user's actual walking velocity; instead it should make the perceived scene-change speed differ from the actual movement speed so that the user perceives natural walking behavior.
[50/S3/F5] The desired perceived scene-change speed, denoted v_visual or v_perceived, is computed by multiplying the visual gain g by the actual instantaneous walking speed v_actual, so v_visual = g × v_actual, where v_actual is the user's movement speed and is related to the instantaneous step frequency.
[50/S3/F6] The visual gain g is typically set to 2.10 because prior studies have identified that value as providing a good visual gain for natural walking behavior.
[50/S3/F7] In the model, the actual walking speed V_actual is calculated as V_actual = f(x) × 0.157 × (h × 1.72)^2, where f(x) is the instantaneous step frequency as a function of distance x and h is the user's height.
[50/S3/F8] The instantaneous step frequency f(x) is computed from the Fourier-series expression f(x) = (4/π) × 1.8 × Σ_{k=0}^{d/2−1} [1/(2k+1) × sin((2k+1)ωx)], where ω = 2πf0, f0 is the initial frequency and equals 1/(2d), and d is the distance between the source and destination.
[50/S4/F0] The natural walking speed model was formed by multiplying computed natural walking behavior by a visual gain factor to let the user perceive natural walking.
[50/S4/F1] The empirical study was carried out to confirm whether the natural walking speed model, described as instantaneous speed, enhances perceived realism compared with a constant average speed.
[50/S4/F2] The empirical study was conducted with 34 participants: 20 male and 14 female, aged 15 to 47 years with a mean age of 29.3 years, and with heights of 159 to 170 centimeters with a mean height of 167.97 centimeters.
[50/S4/F3] The empirical study used a virtual art exhibition environment experienced with an Oculus Rift, in which system-controlled navigation moved the user at constant speed or instantaneous natural walking speed through pictures, with a forced 10-second stop at each picture.
[50/S4/F4] Each participant experienced both constant speed and instantaneous speed conditions in a randomized order, with breaks between the conditions, to avoid practice effect and bias in the subsequent data collection.
[50/S4/F5] The study used two 1-to-10 rating scales, with 1 representing worst and 10 representing the best value, to rate level of realism and user preference, and speed with levels constant and instantaneous was the independent variable, with realism and preference as the dependent variables.
[50/S4/F6] Box plots of the realism and preference ratings showed that instantaneous speed produced higher realism ratings and higher user preference than constant speed, and paired sample t-tests with a p-value of 0.05 showed these differences were statistically significant.
[50/S4/F7] The instantaneous natural walking speed model is suitable for a realistic walking experience in controlled virtual reality (VR) tools and is likely to increase presence.
[50/S4/F8] The instantaneous natural walking speed model alone does not address cyber sickness, and the question of how to minimize cyber sickness with only that model cannot be answered, so additional measures are required.
