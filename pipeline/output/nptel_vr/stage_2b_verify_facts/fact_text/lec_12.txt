[12/S0/F0] Cybersickness (CS) evaluation strategies can be organized into three aspects: input, process, and output.
[12/S0/F1] In machine learning (ML) evaluation of cybersickness (CS), interpretable models are similar to transparent models, offer more flexibility in customization and in how the model is used, and provide more precise details about the relationships between the input and the output.
[12/S0/F2] Interpretable machine learning (ML) algorithms used to measure cybersickness (CS) include CN2 rule induction, bagged decision trees, Naive Bayes classifiers, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and long short-term memory (LSTM) regression analysis.
[12/S0/F3] Combinations of machine learning (ML) techniques have also been used to identify and provide insight into cybersickness (CS); for example, explainable boosting machine (EBM), decision tree, and logistic regression were combined using biophysiological and subjective measurements as input.
[12/S0/F4] Black-box models in cybersickness (CS) evaluation are models that cannot be easily understood or explained; they take an input and produce an output without revealing how or why that output is generated from the input, unlike transparent or interpretable models.
[12/S0/F5] Black-box models studied for cybersickness (CS) evaluation include long short-term memory (LSTM), convolutional neural network (CNN), and support vector machine (SVM) models.
[12/S0/F6] In a study comparing long short-term memory (LSTM), convolutional neural network (CNN), and support vector machine (SVM) models for cybersickness (CS) measurement, LSTM achieved the best results.
[12/S0/F7] Black-box models in cybersickness (CS) evaluation generally provide higher prediction accuracy than transparent and interpretable models, but unlike those other models they require large amounts of data and processing power.
[12/S0/F8] Hybrid models in machine learning (ML) evaluation of cybersickness (CS) are combinations of different machine learning (ML) models, designed to provide high accuracy and transparency in their decision-making process.
[12/S0/F9] Hybrid models used to predict or classify cybersickness (CS) include a combination of a deep embedded self-organizing map (DESOM) and an autoencoder designed from EEGNET, a specialized convolutional neural network (CNN) for electroencephalography (EEG) prediction, and a voting classifier combining random forest, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), logistic regression, and a multilayer perceptron neural network for binary and three-level classification.
[12/S0/F10] Hybrid models in cybersickness (CS) evaluation can combine the strengths of transparent and black-box models by providing good predictive accuracy and some interpretability, making them useful in application domains where both high accuracy and transparency are essential, such as medical diagnosis.
[12/S1/F0] Classification based on the event of measurement is a category of cybersickness (CS) evaluation technique that distinguishes detection, which uses input generated after the experience to determine whether cybersickness has occurred, from prediction, which occurs before the experience to determine whether cybersickness will occur.
[12/S1/F1] Most cybersickness (CS) evaluation work focuses on detection rather than prediction, but prediction is important because it can enable mitigation measures before cybersickness occurs.
[12/S1/F2] Limited research on predicting cybersickness (CS) uses physiological signals and video features, baseline measurements before virtual reality (VR) immersion, and machine learning (ML) studies that predict susceptibility to cybersickness or impending cybersickness episodes.
[12/S1/F3] The outputs of self-report questionnaires for cybersickness (CS) are generally scores or ratings on rating scales, and these scores or ratings can be used to assess the severity of cybersickness.
[12/S1/F4] Machine learning (ML)-based cybersickness (CS) evaluation can produce either classification labels or a numerical sickness score as output.
[12/S1/F5] In machine learning (ML)-based cybersickness (CS) evaluation, classification labels indicate the likelihood or severity of cybersickness, for example sickness or no sickness, or low, moderate, or acute sickness.
[12/S1/F6] In machine learning (ML)-based cybersickness (CS) evaluation, a sickness score quantifies the severity of the cybersickness experience and is generally produced by a regression model.
[12/S2/F0] Because complete prevention of cybersickness (CS) is generally not possible, cybersickness mitigation uses measures to reduce or eliminate cybersickness or at least lessen its impact.
[12/S2/F1] Cybersickness (CS) mitigation involves a trade-off between reducing cybersickness and preserving user experience, because mitigation strategies often affect presence or the user's ability to perceive the virtual world, and presence affects user experience.
[12/S2/F2] Cybersickness (CS) mitigation is considered from two important perspectives, the developer's perspective and the user's perspective, because even a good developer solution may fail to prevent cybersickness if the user does not use the system properly or as expected.
[12/S3/F0] Developer-side strategies for reducing cybersickness (CS) include field of view (FOV) adjustment, dynamic visual blur, rest frames, optical-flow manipulation, hardware-level alterations, and locomotion modifications.
[12/S3/F1] Field of view (FOV) is the amount of virtual environment that is visible at any moment.
[12/S3/F2] Field of view (FOV) adjustment can, ideally, mitigate the onset or severity of cybersickness (CS) because restricting the field of view reduces peripheral optical flow.
[12/S3/F3] Field of view (FOV) adjustment has been found to be effective across genders without any negative impacts.
[12/S3/F4] A drawback of field of view (FOV) adjustment is that it can reduce presence or sense of presence and impair performance in visual tasks because it restricts certain visual content, potentially degrading user experience.
[12/S3/F5] A symmetric mask is a classical field of view (FOV) adjustment technique that occludes the periphery in reaction to translational and/or angular velocity, but it can affect presence.
[12/S3/F6] An asymmetric mask is a field of view (FOV) adjustment strategy that is considered an adaptive restrictor because it obscures peripheral regions with higher optical flow during virtual locomotion while leaving regions with lower optical flow visible, so it adapts based on optical flow.
[12/S3/F7] A side restrictor, described as an asymmetric mask, is a field of view (FOV) adjustment strategy that obscures only one side of the periphery during virtual rotation and laterally shifts the center of restriction in the direction of the turn, providing a controlled way of restriction.
[12/S3/F8] A ground visible restrictor is a field of view (FOV) adjustment strategy in which the ground plane remains visible during movement.
[12/S3/F9] Dynamic visual blur is a technique for reducing the possibility of cybersickness (CS) in which blurring means obscuring or reducing clarity, and it has two types: depth-of-field blur, which uses well-known depth cues in human vision, and peripheral blur, also called dynamic non-salient region blurring.
[12/S3/F10] Rest frames are fixed elements within the virtual environment that remain stationary relative to the real world regardless of the user's movements, and rest frames attached to the head reference frame are known as independent visual backgrounds, with examples including clouds, grids, and a virtual nose.
[12/S3/F11] Optical-flow manipulation is a cybersickness (CS) reduction technique that can involve visually mixing optical-flow patterns in the reverse direction of virtual visual motion, using a restricted field of view (FOV) aligned with optical flow, or reducing perceived optical flow by geometrical simplification of the virtual environment.
[12/S3/F12] Hardware-level alterations can reduce or eliminate cybersickness (CS) by modifying existing hardware to different degrees or by proposing completely new hardware.
[12/S3/F13] Hardware-level alterations for reducing cybersickness (CS) include a sparse peripheral display that expands a head-mounted display (HMD) periphery with a colorful LED matrix, and miniature LED screens placed in the HMD.
[12/S3/F14] Hardware-level alterations for reducing cybersickness (CS) include a Noble camera setup twin cam that reduces motion blur and cybersickness, and a hybrid 360 panoramic video camera rig that can be used to reduce cybersickness.
[12/S3/F15] Phantom legs is a hardware-level alteration that applies alternating haptic cues synchronized to users' footsteps using additional hardware, and it was found to reduce cybersickness (CS) to a great extent.
[12/S3/F16] Floor vibrations are a hardware-level alteration for reducing cybersickness (CS) that uses vibration-driven whole-body tactile feedback to match the vibrations of the virtual environment.
[12/S3/F17] Simultaneous presentation of sound and vibration is a hardware-level alteration that has been tried for reducing cybersickness (CS), for example in a walking vibe system that uses two small vibration motors behind the ears for vibrotactile feedback during a walking experience.
[12/S3/F18] Modified motion platforms are a hardware-level alteration in which short bursts of rotational acceleration matching visual forward acceleration were found quite effective in reducing cybersickness (CS), and the platforms can be modified to produce this effect.
[12/S3/F19] Locomotion techniques can play a crucial role in preventing cybersickness (CS), and they can be modified or new techniques can be proposed to reduce its effect.
[12/S3/F20] Locomotion modifications for reducing cybersickness (CS) include novel walking methods instead of natural walking, teleportation rather than steering-based locomotion, dynamic steering speed that skips certain locations when going over long distances to reduce the duration of virtual exposure, viewpoint snapping that renders moving pictures in discrete chunks during rotation, and invisible ramps placed on top of stairs.
[12/S4/F0] Cybersickness (CS) is subjective and individual susceptibility to cybersickness varies, so a one-size-fits-all approach or developer-side interventions alone may not be sufficient and users need their own countermeasures to reduce the effects of cybersickness.
[12/S4/F1] To reduce the risk of cybersickness (CS), users may be advised to avoid continuous or long-duration exposure to a virtual environment, and studies suggest that the generally safe maximum duration of a virtual reality (VR) session for the majority of users likely to suffer from cybersickness is between 55 and 70 minutes, although the exact duration may vary with device, content, and other factors, and longer exposure is risky.
[12/S4/F2] Habituation is the gradual decrease in the intensity of cybersickness (CS) after repeated experience in a virtual environment.
[12/S4/F3] Users can attempt habituation to a virtual environment through a two-stage approach in which users first use repeated short-duration exposures until undesirable symptoms appear, then use repeated longer-duration exposures after full recovery from cybersickness (CS) symptoms.
[12/S4/F4] Users can try slow diaphragmatic breathing, or controlled breathing patterns, to delay the onset, reduce the severity, and, to a large extent, reduce the possibility of cybersickness (CS).
[12/S4/F5] Playing pleasant background music and ensuring adequate external air flow are user-side strategies that can calm or soothe the mind and reduce cybersickness (CS).
