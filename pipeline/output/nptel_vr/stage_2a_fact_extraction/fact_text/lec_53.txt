[53/S0/F0] The optimal path approach in virtual reality (VR) navigation optimization identifies a path that lets a user explore all points of interest in minimum time, but in a very large virtual world such as the Majuli River Island virtual tour, that minimum time may still be very large compared with the expected user exposure duration, so the approach may still lead to cybersickness.
[53/S0/F1] In a walking-based virtual reality (VR) exploration, the optimal path under a time constraint approach sets an upper time limit, called the time threshold, for the user's exposure to the virtual tour, and within that duration it identifies a path that covers the maximum number of the most important points of interest, where covering includes interacting with or inspecting those points and navigating from one to another.
[53/S0/F2] Majuli is a river island on the Brahmaputra River in the state of Assam, surrounded on all sides by the Brahmaputra River and its tributaries.
[53/S0/F3] Satras on Majuli are monasteries of the Vaisnavite faith that contain tangible and intangible cultural aspects, and there are 22 Satras in total.
[53/S0/F4] Auniati Satra is one of the largest, most prominent, and most visited Satras on Majuli, with a campus covering approximately 1200 square meters and at least 25 points of interest.
[53/S0/F5] Exploring the whole Auniati Satra by walking may take up to a day, including stoppage at the points of interest, and a full-day exposure to a virtual reality (VR) tour is not recommended because it would induce cybersickness.
[53/S0/F6] The system-controlled virtual reality (VR) tour of Auniati Satra uses walking-based exploration with the natural walking speed model to provide a realistic experience.
[53/S0/F7] For the exploration of Auniati Satra in virtual reality (VR), an optimized navigation path can be proposed using either the optimal path approach or the optimal path under a time constraint approach to maximize the user experience.
[53/S1/F0] The Satra campus is modeled as a graph in which the points of interest (POI) are the nodes, the graph has at least 25 nodes, and the edges connecting pairs of nodes are weighted by the Euclidean distance between the corresponding points of interest.
[53/S1/F1] Converting the Satra site into a graph representation is an iterative process that requires detailed field study and revision of an initial graph.
[53/S1/F2] A team collected the Satra graph data through a field study, described as a kind of contextual inquiry, during the Kati Bihu festival, by observing people's travel and stopping behavior throughout the day, manually recording that behavior, and interviewing campus residents and Satra committee members about the places people typically visit and the places interviewees recommended to tourists.
[53/S1/F3] A point of interest (POI) in the Satra graph does not have to refer to an actual physical object; for example, a lake is not treated as a point of interest, but the corners of the lake are.
[53/S1/F4] When the first navigation optimization approach gives a minimum time above the time threshold, the second approach requires each point of interest (POI) node to be assigned a relative importance from discussions with people and an estimated maximum wait time from observed visitor behavior.
[53/S1/F5] The maximum wait time at a point of interest (POI) is the time a user needs to stop at the point of interest, inspect it, interact with it if possible, and resume navigation.
[53/S1/F6] Once the graph is obtained, a traveling salesman problem (TSP) solver can be used to find a minimum-time path covering all 25 nodes, or a vehicle routing problem with profits (VRPP) solver can be used to find a path within a chosen time threshold that covers the maximum number of most important points of interest while accounting for the maximum wait time at each point of interest.
