[10/S0/F0] Cybersickness (CS), also known in the literature as visually induced motion sickness (VIMS), is a condition that can affect individuals when they interact with virtual reality (VR) technology and is primarily relevant to virtual reality.
[10/S0/F1] A large number of symptoms may indicate the onset of cybersickness (CS) or cybersickness itself, and common ones include nausea, headache, excessive sweating, dryness of mouth, fullness of stomach, vomiting or a tendency to vomit, dizziness, disorientation, fatigue, and strange hand-eye coordination in which coordination does not happen in the expected way, so if one or more of these symptoms occur during a virtual reality (VR) experience, cybersickness can be assumed to be happening or going to happen.
[10/S0/F2] Cybersickness (CS) can be caused by many factors, not by a single factor.
[10/S0/F3] Reducing the impact of cybersickness (CS) is very important for usability and acceptability of virtual reality (VR) systems because it greatly enhances overall user experience and makes virtual reality applications more appealing to a wider range of people.
[10/S1/F0] Cyber sickness can be understood through three aspects: causes, evaluation, and mitigation.
[10/S1/F1] The causes of cyber sickness are broadly internal factors and external factors, and external factors comprise hardware and virtual reality (VR) content.
[10/S1/F2] Internal factors for cyber sickness are individual characteristics and conditions inherent to users that can increase the likelihood of experiencing cyber sickness, including age, gender, motion sickness (MS) susceptibility, physical condition, and emotional condition.
[10/S1/F3] Age is related to cyber sickness susceptibility: children aged 2 to 12 may show increased sensitivity, that tendency decreases as they grow older, older persons are likely to experience higher levels, cyber sickness is generally uncommon among young children, and it is highly unlikely in users aged 12 to 25.
[10/S1/F4] Cyber sickness can be independent of age when users view general virtual reality (VR) content, while age may be a factor for controllable content, making the age relationship clearer for controllable content.
[10/S1/F5] The effect of gender on cyber sickness has no conclusive result because studies report contradictory findings: some find that female users have more severe cases, while others find no discernible difference between male and female participants in the presence or severity of cyber sickness.
[10/S1/F6] Female users who use head-mounted displays (HMDs) are associated with increased cyber sickness.
[10/S1/F7] A possible reason for increased cyber sickness among female head-mounted display (HMD) users is inter-pupillary distance (IPD) fit, because IPD determines stereoscopic vision and a greater proportion of females may have trouble finding a good fit; this implies virtual reality (VR) displays should have a wider IPD adjustable range for female users.
[10/S1/F8] Motion sickness (MS) occurs when the brain receives conflicting signals from various sensory organs, causing symptoms like nausea and dizziness that are similar to cyber sickness.
[10/S1/F9] Virtual reality (VR) recreates realistic movement and visual environments, making the user feel as if in a real-world environment; therefore, users with motion sickness (MS) susceptibility tend also to have cyber sickness susceptibility.
[10/S1/F10] Studies indicate a positive correlation between motion sickness (MS) susceptibility and cyber sickness risk, so individuals with higher MS susceptibility are likely to experience more severe cyber sickness symptoms.
[10/S1/F11] Prior exposure to virtual reality (VR) systems and repeated exposure to VR can reduce the effect of motion sickness (MS) susceptibility on cyber sickness and reduce cyber sickness severity over time, so the positive correlation may not hold if either or both conditions are satisfied.
[10/S1/F12] Body awareness, the ability of the user to attend to and perceive bodily sensations, is a physical condition related to cyber sickness; poor sleep quality can affect this awareness and may contribute to or increase the severity of cyber sickness.
[10/S1/F13] People with greater retinal eccentricity may be more susceptible to cyber sickness.
[10/S1/F14] Individuals with neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis may experience more severe cyber sickness symptoms.
[10/S1/F15] A user's physical position affects cyber sickness susceptibility: using a virtual reality (VR) system in a standing posture is likely to induce more cyber sickness, or more severe cyber sickness, than using the same system while sitting.
[10/S1/F16] Emotional conditions at the time of use can increase the likelihood of cyber sickness: negative emotions such as anxiety, stress, and frustration are typically associated with it, and personality traits such as neuroticism may also increase the likelihood.
[10/S2/F0] In virtual reality (VR), an external factor in cybersickness (CS) is a factor that is not subjective to a user's mental or physical condition, and hardware is one category of external factors.
[10/S2/F1] Cybersickness (CS) in virtual reality (VR) is not simply a problem of inferior hardware quality; although early users associated it with inferior hardware, many problems persist with advanced technology, so some aspects of devices themselves induce cybersickness.
[10/S2/F2] Display resolution is a hardware factor that reduces cybersickness (CS) up to a certain point; a study evaluating 1K, 2K, 3K, and 4K resolutions found a substantial reduction in CS induction from 1K to 2K, but no significant further reduction from 2K to 3K or from 3K to 4K.
[10/S2/F3] Latency in virtual reality (VR) is the time delay, or time gap, between a user's movement in the physical world and the corresponding change in the virtual world.
[10/S2/F4] In head-mounted display (HMD)-based virtual reality (VR), cybersickness (CS) increases with latency; the more latency there is, the more likely it is to induce cybersickness.
[10/S2/F5] Latency jitter is variability in latency, meaning that the time gap between a physical-world action and its virtual-world reflection changes from time to time.
[10/S2/F6] Latency jitter causes irregularities in the timing of data delivery and can lead to inconsistencies, discomfort, disorientation, and cybersickness (CS).
[10/S2/F7] Display type is a hardware factor that significantly influences the severity of cybersickness (CS), with users using head-mounted display (HMD)-based or virtual reality (VR) headset displays experiencing more CS than users using conventional monitors or non-immersive VR systems; in one study comparing stereoscopic three-dimensional (3D) videos on flat screens, panoramic screens, and VR headsets, VR headset users experienced more CS.
[10/S2/F8] Users with higher-end head-mounted displays (HMDs) experience less cybersickness (CS) than users with lower-end HMDs, a difference related to display qualities such as resolution, brightness, clarity, and contrast.
[10/S2/F9] In virtual reality (VR), the type of input method used, such as hand controllers, treadmills, and motion sensors, can influence the level of cybersickness (CS).
[10/S2/F10] In virtual reality (VR), motion controllers such as steering with handlebars may cause more cybersickness (CS) than non-motion controls such as a joystick, and a gamepad controller causes less cybersickness than a hand controller.
[10/S3/F0] The causes of cybersickness are broadly divided into internal factors, such as age and gender, and external factors, which are divided into hardware factors, such as display type, latency, latency jitter, resolution, and input technology, and content factors.
[10/S3/F1] Virtual reality (VR) content is an external factor among the causes of cybersickness.
