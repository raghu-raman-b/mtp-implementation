[11/S0/F0] The design of virtual environments can have a significant impact on the likelihood and severity of cyber sickness (CS).
[11/S0/F1] The six virtual reality (VR) content factors that affect cyber sickness (CS) are visual motion, means of locomotion, field of view, type of content, duration, and visual complexity.
[11/S0/F2] Visual motion, the movement experienced by a user in a virtual reality (VR) environment, is considered one of the primary causes of cyber sickness (CS), and higher visual motion can increase the risk of cyber sickness while the way the content moves also affects cyber sickness and its severity.
[11/S0/F3] Vection is the sensation of self-motion, is highest when motion is sensed in peripheral stimuli, and is positively correlated with visual motion stimulus, indicating that visual motion plays a crucial role in the onset and severity of cyber sickness (CS).
[11/S0/F4] The severity of cyber sickness (CS) also significantly increases with larger height and faster speed in a virtual environment.
[11/S0/F5] The viewpoint during motion is also an important factor for cyber sickness (CS).
[11/S0/F6] Brief exposure to turbulent visual motion alone is not enough to induce cyber sickness (CS), but longer exposure to turbulent visual motion can induce cyber sickness.
[11/S0/F7] Cyber sickness (CS) increases with increasing angular velocity and is more intense when rotation takes place around the x and y axes than around the z axis.
[11/S1/F0] Means of locomotion, meaning how a user moves in a virtual world, is a content-related factor in cybersickness (CS), because a sensory mismatch between virtual and physical movements can cause discomfort and disorientation that lead to cybersickness.
[11/S1/F1] Control over motion in a virtual world, meaning whether and how a user controls movement, is an aspect of locomotion that directly affects presence and indirectly affects cybersickness (CS) through presence; greater presence in virtual reality (VR) generally reduces cybersickness, and more control means greater presence and less control means lower presence.
[11/S1/F2] The type and extent of the control mechanism used in virtual reality (VR) determine the likelihood of cybersickness (CS); research studies find that hand-based motion control leads to significantly less cybersickness than whole-body motion control.
[11/S1/F3] The type of transition, meaning how the scene changes from one field of view to another, is another aspect of locomotion that affects cybersickness (CS); teleportation, an instantaneous transition, is found to reduce cybersickness compared with naturalistic free movement such as walking, running, and jumping.
[11/S2/F0] Field of view (FOV) is a content-related factor in virtual reality (VR) that affects susceptibility to cybersickness (CS), and it is the extent to which the display in a VR headset captures the user's field of vision.
[11/S2/F1] A larger field of view (FOV) in virtual reality (VR) captures a larger field of vision and generally results in greater immersion.
[11/S2/F2] Research has found that reducing the field of view (FOV) in virtual reality (VR) can be an effective way to alleviate symptoms of cybersickness (CS).
[11/S2/F3] An excessively narrow field of view (FOV) in virtual reality (VR) can decrease immersion and increase head movement to look for things in the environment, which may exacerbate or increase cybersickness (CS); finding the optimal FOV is crucial for creating a comfortable VR experience.
[11/S2/F4] The type of content is an important factor in cybersickness (CS), and action content, fast movements, and abrupt alterations in a virtual reality (VR) environment are found to induce more CS than neutral and static content.
[11/S2/F5] For users with a high preference for adrenaline sports or adventurous things, exposure to action content, fast movements, or abrupt alterations in a virtual reality (VR) environment may not lead to severe cybersickness (CS) compared with users who are not exposed to such sports or adventures, because they are already accustomed to such behavior and environments.
[11/S2/F6] The genre of a virtual environment affects cybersickness (CS), and content based on horror scenes is likely to induce more CS than pleasant general content.
[11/S2/F7] The geometry and terrain of a virtual environment affect cybersickness (CS); when a user travels over regular bumps and irregular terrain, CS is more likely than when the same travel occurs over flat terrain without bumpy rides.
[11/S2/F8] Weather conditions in a virtual environment affect the induction of cybersickness (CS); stormy conditions are more likely to cause higher CS than clear weather conditions.
[11/S3/F0] Virtual reality (VR) exposure duration is an important factor in cybersickness (CS), and prolonged exposure through head-mounted displays (HMDs) leads to greater cybersickness.
[11/S3/F1] Cybersickness (CS) increases with virtual reality (VR) exposure time when users use different locomotion controllers during navigation tasks in a virtual environment, and the effect of the locomotion technique used to move from one point to another on cybersickness is further complicated by longer duration.
[11/S3/F2] Continuous exposure to live-action 180-degree videos results in higher cybersickness (CS), and the increase in cybersickness grows with exposure time.
[11/S3/F3] Visual complexity is an external factor that is positively correlated with cybersickness (CS) symptoms, so scenes with more objects of different shapes, sizes, and movements are more likely to induce cybersickness.
[11/S4/F0] Measurement techniques for cybersickness (CS) rely on three components: the input provided for measurement, the measurement technique itself, and the output generated.
[11/S4/F1] Inputs for measuring cybersickness (CS) can be questionnaire-based, physiological and biometric information, behavioral information, and content-based information.
[11/S4/F2] The measurement technique or evaluation process for cybersickness (CS) can be manual, automated, machine learning-based, or real-time evaluation while the experience is taking place.
[11/S4/F3] The output of cybersickness (CS) measurement can be binary classes, multiple classes, or a sickness score that is a number.
[11/S4/F4] Questionnaire-based input for measuring cybersickness (CS) is a common method that uses self-report questionnaires or scales, generally in the form of ratings, to rate users' discomfort or disorientation on a numerical scale.
[11/S4/F5] Questionnaire-based cybersickness (CS) scales are easy to administer and help researchers or practitioners better understand the causes of cybersickness and identify potential interventions.
[11/S4/F6] Questionnaire-based cybersickness (CS) scales are typically administered before or after an experience, can also be administered during the experience, with before and after being the most common timing and during being occasional.
[11/S4/F7] The Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) is one of the most extensively used questionnaires for measuring cybersickness (CS).
[11/S4/F8] The Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) contains 16 items divided into three subscales: the oculomotor subscale, the disorientation subscale, and the nausea subscale.
[11/S4/F9] In the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ), participants rate the intensity of individual symptoms belonging to the oculomotor, disorientation, and nausea subscales on a scale from 0 to 3, where a higher score denotes more severe symptoms.
[11/S4/F10] The Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) was not originally designed for virtual reality (VR) but was adapted for use in the context of virtual reality.
[11/S4/F11] Questionnaires specifically designed for virtual reality (VR) include the Virtual Reality Sickness Questionnaire (VRSQ) and the Cyber Sickness Questionnaire (CSQ), which is good for evaluating virtual reality experiences with head-mounted displays (HMDs).
[11/S4/F12] Shorter and more concise questionnaires used to assess specific conditions include the Misery Scale (MIS) and the Fast Motion Sickness Scale (FMS).
[11/S4/F13] Questionnaires used to measure susceptibility to cybersickness (CS) include the Motion History Questionnaire (MHQ), the Motion Sickness Susceptibility Questionnaire (MS-SQ), and the Visually Induced Motion Sickness Susceptibility Questionnaire (VIMS or VIMS-SQ).
[11/S4/F14] Questionnaires commonly used to assess overall discomfort include the Nausea Scale and the Motion Sickness Assessment Questionnaire (MSAQ).
[11/S5/F0] Physiological or biometric inputs can be used to detect cybersickness (CS) because questionnaire-based inputs can be subjective, are prone to inaccuracies, and may not always be reliable.
[11/S5/F1] Electroencephalogram (EEG) is one of the widely used physiological or biometric inputs for detecting cybersickness (CS).
[11/S5/F2] Physiological or biometric inputs for detecting cybersickness (CS) include heart rate, galvanic skin response (GSR), electroencephalogram (EEG), electrocardiogram (ECG), electrogastrogram (EGG), electrooculography (EOG), respiration signals, photoplethysmography (PPG), and electromyogram (EMG).
[11/S6/F0] Behavioral inputs for measuring cybersickness (CS) include blink rate, head and body movement, the manner of those movements, and postural sway.
[11/S6/F1] Postural sway can be monitored for measuring cybersickness (CS) during virtual reality (VR) exposure using axial movements or changes in the user's center of pressure.
[11/S6/F2] Postural instability is positively correlated with the severity of cybersickness (CS), so postural sway can indicate CS severity.
[11/S6/F3] Content-based inputs for measuring cybersickness (CS) include optical flow, depth cues, and exceptional motion velocity.
[11/S6/F4] Optical flow is the apparent motion perceived by virtual reality (VR) users due to relative motion between the user and the environment.
[11/S6/F5] The four categories of inputs for measuring cybersickness (CS) are questionnaire-based inputs, which are subjective; physiological and biometric inputs, which are objective; behavioral inputs; and content-based inputs.
[11/S7/F0] Cybersickness (CS) measurement techniques based on input are broadly of two types: manual evaluation, which is based on human intervention, and machine learning-based (ML) or automated evaluation, which uses automated algorithms on input to measure cybersickness.
[11/S7/F1] Manual evaluation for cybersickness (CS) is entirely based on human intervention and typically uses questionnaire-based assessment, in which a person distributes the questionnaire, collects feedback, and reviews the responses to decide the severity, onset, or possibility of cybersickness.
[11/S7/F2] Machine learning-based (ML) evaluation of cybersickness (CS) can use psychological, physiological, or biometric inputs to train models that automatically determine cybersickness.
[11/S7/F3] One way to perform machine learning-based (ML) evaluation of cybersickness (CS) is to use transparent models, which provide clear insight into the decision-making process and whose inner workings can easily be understood.
[11/S7/F4] Stepwise regression analysis can be considered a transparent model that is utilized to measure cybersickness (CS) using a variety of physiological signals or physiological inputs.
