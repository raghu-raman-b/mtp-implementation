[3/S0/F1] Augmented reality (AR), unlike virtual reality (VR), does not require an immersive experience and instead emphasizes augmenting real-world content with digital content.
[3/S0/F2] Augmented reality (AR) applications include archaeology, architecture, industrial manufacturing, commerce (particularly e-commerce), gaming, and medical domains, among other domains.
[3/S0/F3] In archaeology, augmented reality (AR) can display things that are not visible to the naked eye on a screen and can reconstruct lost structures.
[3/S0/F4] In industrial manufacturing, augmented reality (AR) can allow a person to see things while repairing or troubleshooting a component of an industrial setup.
[3/S0/F5] The presence of many augmented reality (AR) applications in commerce, particularly e-commerce, is one motivation for the metaverse concept.
[3/S0/F6] Pokemon Go is a famous augmented reality (AR) gaming application.
[3/S1/F0] The Gatwick Airport passenger app used more than 2,000 beacons placed at different points in the passenger terminal to tell a passenger their exact location and the direction to move to reach a desired terminal, and such apps are helpful in large terminals.
[3/S1/F1] Augmented reality (AR) customer-experience applications that let a customer preview a product before purchase include the IKEA Place app, which lets a user scan a room and place IKEA objects in the digital image of the room to see how the room would look, and a Dulux visualizer app, which lets a customer digitally paint walls on a screen and see the effect before deciding whether to buy the paint.
[3/S1/F2] An augmented reality (AR) virtual tape measure application can measure the heights and lengths of objects inside or outside a room using a mobile phone, so a physical tape is not needed.

[3/S2/F0] Augmented reality (AR) applications are categorized by technology rather than by psychological or immersive experience into two broad categories: marker-based augmented reality applications and markerless augmented reality applications.
[3/S2/F1] Markerless augmented reality (AR) applications can have four subcategories: location-based, superimposition-based, projection-based, and outlining-based.
[3/S2/F2] Marker-based augmented reality (AR) applications use markers placed in the environment to trigger an augmented experience, and when a device detects a marker, the desired digital content appears at that marker's position in the real-world view.
[3/S2/F3] Markers used in marker-based augmented reality (AR) applications include quick response (QR) codes, simple color codes, color bars, and fiducial markers.
[3/S2/F4] Marker-based augmented reality (AR) applications are easier to build but difficult to maintain because markers are prone to damage, and a damaged marker may make the application not work.
[3/S2/F5] Markerless augmented reality (AR) applications are more versatile than marker-based augmented reality applications, do not require physical markers, can use computer vision techniques, and allow users to decide where to place virtual objects.
[3/S2/F6] Location-based markerless augmented reality (AR) applications rely on a location sensor, such as a global positioning system (GPS) sensor, to detect the user's location with respect to a reference frame, and digital content is augmented on the real-world view when the user is at a specific location.
[3/S2/F7] Superimposition-based markerless augmented reality (AR) applications recognize one or multiple physical objects using computer vision techniques and superimpose digital content on the real-world scene depending on the recognized objects.
[3/S2/F8] Projection-based markerless augmented reality (AR) applications use light projectors to project digital graphics, digital characters, or other digital content onto an object, a surface, or a space, such as in holographic projection.
[3/S2/F9] Outlining-based markerless augmented reality (AR) applications use object recognition to recreate the user's immediate surroundings and digitally enhance the view, for example by showing cars around a driver that are not clearly visible in low-light conditions.
[3/S3/F0] Virtual reality (VR) system categories are based on the experience or psychological aspect, and comprise non-immersive, semi-immersive, and fully immersive systems.
[3/S3/F1] Augmented reality (AR) system categories are based primarily on the technology used to develop and deploy the systems rather than on the psychological aspect, and broadly comprise marker-based and markerless systems.
[3/S3/F2] Marker-based augmented reality (AR) systems are easier to make because they rely on simple algorithms, but their markers are difficult to maintain unless the environment is very sanitized, such as inside a museum; without a well-attended environment, markers in outdoor settings are prone to damage.
[3/S3/F3] Markerless augmented reality (AR) techniques are used to overcome the difficulty of maintaining markers in marker-based systems.
[3/S3/F4] Markerless augmented reality (AR) techniques rely primarily on computer vision and may rely on other sensors, such as global positioning system (GPS) sensors, in location-based applications.
[3/S3/F5] Computer vision-based augmented reality (AR) applications include superimposition-based and outline-based applications.
[3/S3/F6] Projection-based techniques, such as holographic projection, can be used in augmented reality (AR) application development.
[3/S3/F7] Virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) have broadly similar application domains, but VR applications are characterized as immersive, whereas AR applications emphasize augmenting digital content onto the real-world view rather than immersive applications.
