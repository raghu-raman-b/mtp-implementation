[15/S0/F0] Augmented reality (AR) devices can be categorised into headsets and glasses, handheld devices, projection-based systems, spatial augmented reality (spatial AR), head-up displays, and AR contact lenses.
[15/S0/F1] Examples of augmented reality (AR) headsets, smart glasses, and handheld devices include Microsoft HoloLens as a headset, Google Glass and Vuzix Blade Glasses as smart glasses, and smartphones, tablets, and Nintendo devices as handheld devices.
[15/S0/F2] Examples of augmented reality (AR) projection-based systems include projection keyboards, interactive projectors, AR sandboxes, industrial AR systems, and medical systems, while examples of spatial augmented reality (spatial AR) include interactive projectors, AR sandboxes, projection mapping software, laser projectors, and projection keyboards, with interactive projectors and projection keyboards appearing under both categories.
[15/S0/F3] Augmented reality (AR) head-up displays are typically found in cars.
[15/S0/F4] Augmented reality (AR) contact lenses are the latest technology being explored for AR systems, with ongoing research by Google, Innovega, Mojo Vision's Mojo Lens, and Innovega's iOptic system.
[15/S0/F5] The application domains of augmented reality (AR) device categories include headsets and smart glasses in education, retail, healthcare, tourism, and manufacturing; handheld devices in education, retail, e-commerce, tourism, and real estate; projection-based systems and spatial augmented reality (spatial AR) in industrial design, healthcare, training, retail, gaming, education, art and design, medical training, industrial applications, and entertainment; head-up displays in gaming experiences, medicine, wearables, and sports; and AR contact lenses in medical development.
[15/S1/F0] Generally, augmented reality (AR) headsets are larger and more immersive, present more content in the form of real-world content and augmented digital content, and provide a wider field of view.
[15/S1/F1] Augmented reality (AR) headsets typically include built-in sensors, often cameras and depth sensors, and some of these sensors may or may not also be present in virtual reality (VR) headsets.
[15/S1/F2] The Microsoft HoloLens is one example of an augmented reality (AR) headset, and other such headsets are available.
[15/S1/F3] The Microsoft HoloLens includes two depth cameras, one noise-cancelling microphone, a speaker, a port for attaching headphones, special glasses that project digital content onto the regular real-world view, a tilt mechanism, nose pads, one camera, one projector, sensors, a high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) optical head-mounted display (HMD), and spatial audio capabilities.
[15/S1/F4] Virtual reality (VR) headsets may not include some advanced features of augmented reality (AR) headsets and typically have fewer components than an AR headset such as the Microsoft HoloLens, because VR does not need to provide a real-world view.
[15/S1/F5] The Microsoft HoloLens integrates high-definition holograms or digital content with the real-world view obtained through its special glasses.
[15/S1/F6] Interaction in the Microsoft HoloLens takes place through eye movements, voice commands, and hand motions, and the device is equipped with detection of eye movements, voice commands, and hand gestures.
[15/S2/F0] Compared with augmented reality (AR) headsets, AR smart glasses are lighter, less immersive, wearable devices that resemble standard eyeglasses, usually have a smaller field of view, are less powerful, and are more comfortable to wear for extended periods, whereas AR headsets are more powerful wearables.
[15/S2/F1] Augmented reality (AR) smart glasses provide simpler heads-up display-type AR digital content overlays rather than the full 3D holograms provided by AR headsets, because AR headsets contain more components, sensors, and projectors than AR smart glasses.
[15/S2/F2] Google Glass is an earlier example of augmented reality (AR) smart glasses that presents data in a format similar to a smartphone, operates hands-free, and supports interaction through natural-language voice commands, with built-in detection of those commands.
[15/S2/F3] Google Glass includes a gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer, ambient light sensor, proximity sensor, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, speaker, trackpad, microphone, camera, and projector, but its projector and camera are less powerful than similar components in an augmented reality (AR) headset.
[15/S2/F4] Vuzix's Blade is another example of augmented reality (AR) smart glasses that includes tracking sensors and output actuators, including dual haptic feedback components, a display engine, a see-through display, noise-cancelling microphones, Android operating system (OS), memory storage, a camera, universal serial bus (USB) connectivity, a touchpad, and a place to store batteries.
[15/S2/F5] Additional examples of augmented reality (AR) smart glasses include Epson glasses and Magic Leap glasses.
[15/S2/F6] Handheld augmented reality (AR) devices are familiar devices such as smartphones or tablets and are the most common and accessible devices used in AR.
[15/S2/F7] Handheld augmented reality (AR) devices use their own camera to capture a live real-world image that is displayed on the device screen, then overlay digital content on top of that live real-world view.
[15/S2/F8] Handheld augmented reality (AR) devices typically rely on two main augmented reality technologies: marker-based AR and markerless AR.
[15/S3/F0] Spatial augmented reality (AR) devices are the third category of augmented reality devices, also known as room-scale augmented reality or tabletop augmented reality, and they typically use projectors to display graphical information on physical objects; unlike headsets, glasses, or handheld devices, they overlay digital content on physical objects in the real world rather than on the user's view of the real world.
[15/S3/F1] A defining feature of spatial augmented reality (AR) devices is that their display element is not tied to or held by the user, unlike headsets, glasses, or handheld devices, and they can provide more natural and intuitive interfaces than these other device categories.
[15/S3/F2] Spatial augmented reality (AR) devices allow multiple users to interact simultaneously with digital content augmented onto a physical object, whereas with headsets, glasses, or handheld devices each user interacts only with the content on that user's own device and others cannot interact with it.
[15/S3/F3] Major application areas for spatial augmented reality (AR) devices and systems include education, art and design, medical training, industrial applications, museums and exhibits, and entertainment.
[15/S3/F4] The fundamental principle of a spatial augmented reality (AR) system is that it augments the physical environment by adding virtual objects that seem to coexist in the same space as real objects, so the digitally augmented content appears to coexist with the physical space surrounding the user.
[15/S3/F5] Implementing a spatial augmented reality (AR) system requires understanding the geometry and lighting conditions of the physical environment for seamless mixing, which can be achieved using advanced depth sensors or computer vision techniques typically included in the system.
[15/S3/F6] Projection-based augmented reality (AR) systems and spatial augmented reality systems generally refer to similar ideas.
[15/S3/F7] Examples of spatial augmented reality (AR) systems discussed as major categories include interactive projectors, air sandboxes, projection keyboards, and projection mapping.
[15/S3/F8] An interactive projector is an example of a spatial augmented reality (AR) system that projects images or interfaces onto any surface, turning the surface into an interactive display; for example, a projector can project an interface onto a tabletop, making the tabletop an interactive display and an augmented reality experience, and Sony Xperia Touch is an example.
[15/S3/F9] An air sandbox is an example of a spatial augmented reality (AR) system that uses a projector and a sensor, such as a three-dimensional (3D) camera like Microsoft Kinect, to project a topographic map onto a physical box of sand and update the map in real time when users move the sand or gesture, allowing two to three users to interact simultaneously.
[15/S3/F10] A projection keyboard is an example of a spatial augmented reality (AR) system that projects a virtual keyboard onto a flat surface, converting that surface into an interactive keyboard, and it may use infrared sensors to detect finger positions, which is the more common way to handle key-press interaction.
[15/S3/F11] Projection mapping technology is a software-based application of spatial augmented reality (AR) in which software solutions such as TouchDesigner, MadMapper, or HeavyM are used with standard projectors to create complex projections onto three-dimensional (3D) space without a surface, for example projecting a map or a model of the Earth in air.
[15/S3/F12] Projection mapping displays can be interacted with by rotating, pinching, zooming in, or zooming out.
[15/S3/F13] Projection mapping is commonly used in entertainment and gaming industries.
[15/S4/F0] A head-up display (HUD) is an augmented reality (AR) display category that projects images onto a transparent surface, usually a screen or visor; ideally the surface is invisible and only the content is visible, but borders or shades may be visible.
[15/S4/F1] Head-up displays (HUDs) are commonly used in cars and aircraft.
[15/S4/F2] In augmented reality (AR) hardware, head-mounted displays (HMDs), glasses, handheld devices, and spatial AR systems are bulky or require elaborate setups, and smartphones and tablets can be difficult to hold for a long time.
[15/S4/F3] Augmented reality (AR) contact lenses are an emerging display category and a future wearable technology; they are very small, can be placed directly on the eye like a regular contact lens, are not visible to outsiders, and are still in the realm of the future.
[15/S4/F4] Augmented reality (AR) contact lenses superimpose digital images or information onto the wearer's field of vision directly on the lens.
[15/S4/F5] Augmented reality (AR) contact lenses can contain miniaturized components placed on the lens, including imagers made on complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology, a display, wireless components, motion sensors, and other sophisticated components.
[15/S4/F6] Augmented reality (AR) contact lenses can be used to show information such as flight and seat information in front of the wearer while the wearer sees the real world, and may provide ways to interact with or change that information.
[15/S4/F7] Examples of current activities developing augmented reality (AR) contact lenses include Google's smart contact lens project, InnoVega's eye optic system, and Mojo Vision's Mojo Lens.
[15/S4/F8] The effect of augmented reality (AR) contact lenses on the user is not well known or researched; seeing a lot of changing information directly in front of the eyes may affect normal behavior or cause some sort of sickness.
