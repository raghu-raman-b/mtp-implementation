[48/S0/F0] In the requirement gathering stage of interactive system development, functional and non-functional requirements, including usability requirements, are collected from end users and clients, who may be the same but are usually different; usability requirements primarily come from end users, other non-functional requirements may come from clients, and functional requirements may come from both clients and end users.
[48/S0/F1] The interface design part of interactive system development uses a design-prototype-evaluate cycle comprising design, prototyping, and quick evaluation of the prototype; design may be based on intuition, design guidelines, or team brainstorming, quick evaluation may use methods such as cognitive walkthrough or heuristic evaluation, and the cycle is repeated until a stable interface design is reached, meaning further evaluation is unlikely to produce many new issues.
[48/S0/F2] Code design in interactive system development requires producing a code design through iterations and brainstorming, using design languages or notations such as data flow diagram (DFD), entity relationship (ER), object-oriented approaches, and unified modeling language (UML).
[48/S0/F3] Implementation in interactive system development involves coding followed by code testing; primary-level testing may be review-based, secondary-level testing may be more systematic or structured, such as black box or white box testing, and testing can be performed at unit levels, then integration, and then system-level testing.
[48/S0/F4] Code testing shows that a system is executable, but it does not show whether the system is usable; usability testing or an empirical study is therefore needed to estimate usability, although usability was expected because the design-prototype-evaluate cycle was followed.
[48/S0/F5] An empirical study in interactive system development requires identifying participants, setting up the study, and obtaining participants' time.
[48/S0/F6] In the traditional interactive system development lifecycle, the requirement gathering stage may span months; the design-prototype-evaluate stage can take months, although each cycle may take one or two days and, considering repeated cycles and other activities, may take from a couple of weeks to a month; code design may take months or at least a couple of weeks; code testing may take weeks; and an empirical study may take weeks.
[48/S0/F7] The traditional interactive system development lifecycle is rigid in requiring all activities and requirements in a stage to be completed before the next stage starts and before deployment; for example, all requirements must be identified before the requirement gathering stage is complete and all units must be coded before the coding stage is sent to testing; this strict stage completion can lead to time and cost overruns and even non-acceptance by the client.
[48/S0/F8] In the traditional interactive system development lifecycle, deployment is reached only after all requirements are identified, coding is done, code is tested, and usability is tested; because each stage can take a long time, deployment may take a very long time.
[48/S0/F9] In the standard interactive system development lifecycle, each stage requires a long time not only because of executing the stage but also because documentation for the stage must be prepared.
[48/S0/F10] In the standard interactive system development lifecycle, the five documents required before deployment are a feasibility study report, a software requirement specification (SRS) for the requirement gathering, analysis, and specification stage, design documents for interface and interaction design and for code, a code testing document, and a usability study report.
[48/S1/F0] Agile development is a development framework or approach that encourages fast development in practice, starts small by not trying to do everything from the beginning, does not collect all requirements or develop and test the entire system from the beginning, eliminates unnecessary elements and documentation, and ensures quick turnaround of projects that are more acceptable to clients.
[48/S1/F1] Agile development keeps delivering by adding features over successive deliveries, unlike the standard Interactive System Development Life Cycle (ISDLC) method, where the system is first completed and then delivered.
[48/S1/F2] The agile development process flow consists of conceptualization, inception (creation of the team and arranging funds and resources), iteration or sprint (the development cycle, which includes all the other stages of the Interactive System Development Life Cycle (ISDLC)), release (testing, training, and documentation, and deployment), production (ongoing support and maintenance), and retirement (end-of-life activities); conceptualization and inception together roughly refer to physical feasibility study and requirement gathering.
[48/S1/F3] An agile iteration or sprint typically requires four to six weeks, whereas the whole Interactive System Development Life Cycle (ISDLC) may take six to nine months, because the sprint does not try to do everything.
[48/S1/F4] An agile sprint includes requirements, development, testing, and delivery: requirements are based on the product sprint backlog and customer and stakeholder feedback, then development occurs, then testing including training and documentation, then delivery to production.
[48/S1/F5] To deliver within an agile sprint, the development team holds daily meetings or brainstorming, provides a live demonstration after each iteration, and regularly collects quick feedback from stakeholders and customers, which is shared with the team before the next sprint so that it can be addressed; this keeps the team agile by making changes based on feedback as quickly as possible.
[48/S1/F6] Popular agile methods or frameworks include Scrum (SCRUM), Extreme Programming, Disciplined Agile Delivery (DAD), and Dynamic System Development Method (DSDM).
[48/S1/F7] Agile methods provide a better way to develop acceptable products for practical purposes, whereas the Interactive System Development Life Cycle (ISDLC) is primarily meant for understanding the overall flow and for pedagogical purposes rather than for building practical systems within a reasonable time frame.
[48/S2/F0] The book Human-Computer Interaction, User-Centric Computing for Design and the NPTEL course on the design and implementation of human-computer interfaces, specifically weeks 4, 5, and 10 to 12, are recommended for further study of concepts such as prototype building, prototype evaluation, empirical study, and agile methods.
