[42/S0/F0] In the interactive system development lifecycle, empirical evaluation, also called usability evaluation, is the evaluation of the whole system from the point of view of usability.
[42/S0/F1] The interactive system development lifecycle is equally applicable to extended reality (XR) systems.
[42/S0/F2] The interactive system development lifecycle consists of a feasibility study stage, a requirement gathering, analysis and specification stage, a cycle of design, prototyping, and quick evaluation, then code design, coding and implementation, and code testing, and finally an empirical study or usability evaluation stage after the code is bug-free or runs smoothly.
[42/S0/F3] In the interactive system development lifecycle, the design, prototype, and quick evaluation stages form a cycle in which the design is refined based on evaluation results and repeated until the design stabilizes.
[42/S0/F4] Code design, coding and implementation, code testing, and code or system evaluation in interactive system development are standard software development lifecycle (SDLC) stages with nothing special or distinct for virtual reality or augmented reality system design or implementation.
[42/S0/F5] Besides the design-prototype-evaluate cycle, the interactive system development lifecycle can have cycles between code design, coding and implementation, and code testing; after empirical usability evaluation, back to the design-prototype-evaluate cycle or interface design; and between the design-prototype-evaluate cycle and requirement gathering.
[42/S0/F6] In the interactive system development lifecycle, cycles should generally be kept infrequent because frequent cycles lead to cost and time overrun, although the design-prototype-evaluate cycle may be frequent.
[42/S1/F0] The design-prototype-evaluate cycle in experience design consists of designing, quickly prototyping the design, evaluating it, finding problems, refining the design, and repeating this with new prototypes and evaluations, which may require many cycles before the design is stabilized.
[42/S1/F1] In the design-prototype-evaluate cycle, the term design refers only to interface and interaction design, not code design.
[42/S1/F2] A quick usability evaluation technique is required in the design-prototype-evaluate cycle so that the many possible cycles can be accommodated within a reasonable timeframe without cost overrun and so that design ideas, once implemented, lead to a usable system; if usability evaluation is not done quickly, turnaround time increases and leads to cost overrun.
[42/S1/F3] Quick evaluation of design ideas in the design-prototype-evaluate cycle requires both a prototype that models or demonstrates the design idea and can be built quickly, and a quick evaluation method for testing the prototype; both requirements are equally important for a quick execution of the cycle.
[42/S2/F0] Prototyping techniques are used for quick prototyping and quick evaluation of a prototype, and they are typically used to collect feedback on the usability of design ideas.
[42/S2/F1] Design guidelines are only a starting point for a design and do not indicate whether the design is good, bad, usable, or unusable, so the design needs to be implemented and tested to determine whether it will work.
[42/S2/F2] A prototype is a lightweight implementation, with much lower requirements for resources, skilled manpower, and cost than a full implementation, that is used to test whether a design idea will work in practice, so one does not always need to fully implement the idea first.
[42/S2/F3] A full implementation of a software idea involves highly skilled manpower, specifically programmers, and many cost components, including setting up or procuring resources and engaging the manpower, so if the idea is later found not good, the money and time invested are wasted.
[42/S2/F4] A prototype can be discarded after it has served its purpose of testing design ideas, or, in the best case, it can be incrementally refined and tested as development progresses until it evolves into the fully implemented final product.
[42/S3/F0] Prototypes can be broadly considered in two categories, horizontal prototypes and vertical prototypes.
[42/S3/F1] A horizontal prototype is a prototype that depicts the entire interface at a surface level, showing how the interface appears to the user in terms of look, feel, and appearance, without any functionality or interaction, so no real work can be done with it.
[42/S3/F2] Horizontal prototypes are suitable for discussing, brainstorming, and eliciting user feedback about the look, feel, and appearance of an interface, including whether its appearance is understandable, intuitive, and usable.
[42/S3/F3] A vertical prototype primarily represents interactions or functionalities by implementing a few selected features likely to be most commonly used in practice, in depth from the first screen to the last screen after the last action is performed, not all functionalities, because that would amount to having the full system.
[42/S3/F4] Vertical prototypes are suitable for analysis of interactions and features because they implement interactions.
[42/S3/F5] In the description of a vertical prototype, the term implemented and the term screen are generic, so implemented does not necessarily mean writing code, and screen can refer to a two-dimensional (2D) computer screen or a scene in a virtual or immersive environment.
[42/S4/F0] A low-fidelity prototype is a prototype created without technological intervention, programming, code, or a computer, using non-digital materials such as paper, cardboard, or wood, and paper mockups of the look, feel, and functionality of an interface are a popular example.
[42/S4/F1] Low-fidelity prototyping provides a quick and cheap way to make and modify prototypes compared with approaches that require coding and computers, and it is used primarily to brainstorm alternative designs and obtain user feedback on usability.
[42/S4/F2] Low-fidelity prototyping is intuitively primarily associated with horizontal prototyping, but it is not limited to horizontal prototypes and can also be used to implement vertical prototypes.
[42/S4/F3] An interface sketch is a simple drawing that can be made on paper and depicts the major components of an interface, including the screen elements the user sees, buttons, selection options, their appearance, and their layout, so that an interface idea can be expressed to others or users for comments and feedback and its appearance can be envisioned.
[42/S4/F4] A single interface sketch is primarily used to represent a horizontal prototype and is not sufficient to prototype interactions because it does not show the sequence of user actions and the corresponding interface changes needed to execute a task.
[42/S4/F5] To represent interaction, a low-fidelity prototype needs a series of interface sketches rather than a single sketch, a practice often called storyboarding; a single sketch gives a horizontal prototype, whereas a series of sketches can give a vertical prototype depicting interaction.
[42/S4/F6] In a storyboard, each sketch is a keyframe, which is a snapshot of the interaction at a particular point in time; the keyframes visualize the interaction for a specific task and its corresponding scenes, and the storyboard captures the sequence of user actions and the screen appearances the user sees from the beginning of the task to its end.
[42/S4/F7] The overall purpose of a prototype is to convert an idea into a prototype, evaluate it, and revise the idea and prototype if evaluation finds issues; consequently, an interface sketch need not be replicated exactly in the final design, and feedback can change interface elements, their appearance, position, or layout.
[42/S5/F0] A medium-fidelity prototyping approach uses a computer to create simple prototypes without involving coding, whereas a low-fidelity prototyping approach does not rely on computers or digital media.
[42/S5/F1] A high-fidelity prototyping approach uses computer programs or programming to create a prototype, so it is effectively actual software development, maybe on a reduced scale.
[42/S5/F2] High-fidelity prototyping is more sophisticated and requires much more effort than low- or medium-fidelity prototyping, including skilled programmers with programming expertise.
[42/S5/F3] In medium-fidelity prototyping, a computer used only to create static interface sketches produces a horizontal prototype because no real functionality is present, while an Adobe Flash animation video or a Microsoft PowerPoint (PPT) slideshow with simple key presses or timers can simulate interaction and create a vertical prototype.
[42/S5/F4] Low-fidelity prototypes are primarily meant for horizontal prototypes, medium-fidelity approaches are primarily meant for both horizontal and vertical prototypes, and high-fidelity approaches are primarily meant for vertical prototypes, so making horizontal prototypes using a high-fidelity approach may not make much sense.
[42/S6/F0] The Wizard of Oz approach is a prototype-creation technique in which a human subject believes they are interacting with an autonomous computer, but an unseen human operator, called the wizard, operates the computer and returns the requested response or result to the subject.
[42/S6/F1] International Business Machines (IBM) used the Wizard of Oz approach in its 1984 listening typewriter experiment to test the acceptability of a speech recognition system idea before committing resources to build a speech recognizer, because speech recognizer technology was not yet available.
[42/S6/F2] The 1984 International Business Machines (IBM) listening typewriter Wizard of Oz setup consisted of a human user speaking into a microphone while facing a computer that the user believed was a real computer, a hidden wizard separated from the user by a wall, and two computers connected through an intermediary.
[42/S6/F3] The hidden wizard in the 1984 International Business Machines (IBM) listening typewriter Wizard of Oz setup heard the user's speech through the microphone that was also connected to a computer speaker, typed the corresponding characters, and the characters were transmitted to the user's screen, making the user believe that the speech had been automatically converted, although no automatic technology existed at that time to convert the speech into the displayed text.
