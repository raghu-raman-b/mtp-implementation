[57/S0/F0] Realistic scene generation in virtual reality (VR) is more important than in augmented reality (AR) and requires more resources in virtual reality than in augmented reality.
[57/S0/F1] The fourth stage of the three-dimensional (3D) graphics pipeline is view transformation, which is also called the viewing pipeline.
[57/S0/F2] The basic idea of view transformation, which is the viewing pipeline, is that an image on a screen or head-mounted display (HMD) is produced by selecting a portion of a three-dimensional (3D) scene and projecting the portion onto a two-dimensional (2D) display or view plane.
[57/S0/F3] The viewing pipeline consists of a viewing transformation that defines a view coordinate system and transforms a scene from a three-dimensional (3D) world coordinate system to the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system, a projection transformation that projects the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate scene to a two-dimensional (2D) view plane or two-dimensional (2D) view coordinate system, a window-to-viewport transformation that maps the projected scene to a two-dimensional (2D) device coordinate space, and the operations clipping and hidden surface removal, which in the described pipeline take place in the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system.
[57/S0/F4] The sequence of stages or sub-stages in the viewing pipeline is not fixed, because different implementations can alter the sequence.
[57/S1/F0] The world coordinate system is the reference frame in which all scene points are defined.
[57/S1/F1] The three-dimensional (3D) viewing transformation transforms a 3D world coordinate description of a scene to a 3D view coordinate description to simulate looking at the scene with the camera viewing mechanism, and it is performed after defining a view coordinate system from the camera parameters.
[57/S1/F2] The camera parameters used to set up the view coordinate system are the camera position, represented as an origin vector in the world coordinate system; the view-up point, represented as a vector; and the center of interest, also called the look-at point.
[57/S1/F3] The view coordinate system is defined by three mutually orthogonal unit basis vectors N, V, and U, where N is the unit basis vector opposite the looking direction, obtained from the camera origin vector and the look-at point; V is the unit basis vector along the view-up direction, obtained from the vector from the origin point to the view-up point; and U is the cross product of V and N.
[57/S1/F4] Once the view coordinate system is defined, the three-dimensional (3D) transformation from world coordinates to view coordinates is performed by translation and rotation, using a composite transformation matrix that is the product of the rotation and translation transformation matrices; a point in view coordinates is obtained by multiplying the corresponding world-coordinate point vector by this matrix, and the matrices are formed from the view-coordinate axis vectors and their scalar components.
[57/S2/F0] Projection transformation maps objects described in a three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system to a two-dimensional (2D) view plane.
[57/S2/F1] The view plane used in projection is usually a rectangular area, also called a clipping window.
[57/S2/F2] A view volume is a three-dimensional (3D) region in the scene that defines the portion of the scene that is projected onto the view plane, so objects inside the view volume are projected and objects outside it are discarded.
[57/S2/F3] Clipping is the process of discarding objects that are part of the scene but outside the view volume, and it takes place before projection.
[57/S2/F4] Perspective projection is a projection type in which the projectors from an object in the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system to the view plane are not parallel and converge at a center of projection, also called a point of projection; it produces realistic rendering and is used when a realistic scene is to be generated.
[57/S2/F5] Parallel projection is a projection type in which the projectors from the object to the view plane are parallel to each other, so their center of projection or point of projection is at infinity and they never meet.
[57/S2/F6] For parallel projection, the view volume is a rectangular parallelepiped-shaped region, and for perspective projection, the view volume is a frustum, with one face or side of the view volume serving as the clipping window on which objects inside the view volume are projected.
[57/S2/F7] The naive clipping method computes the intersection of view volume boundaries with object boundaries, and this calculation is computationally intensive for arbitrarily shaped view volumes such as frustums.
[57/S2/F8] A canonical view volume is a normalized unit cube into which any arbitrarily defined view volume for parallel or perspective projection is converted in order to simplify pipeline computations.
[57/S2/F9] For parallel projection, a canonical view volume is obtained by scaling an arbitrarily defined view volume into a unit cube.
[57/S2/F10] For perspective projection, a canonical view volume is obtained by converting the frustum into a normalized unit cube, which requires scaling and shearing operations to change its size and shape and involves more computation than the parallel case.
[57/S2/F11] A parallel projection is computed by multiplying a point vector in the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system by a four-by-four parallel projection matrix, in which D is the distance of the clipping window from the origin of the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system, and then dividing the obtained homogeneous coordinate values by the homogeneous factor w, which is typically not equal to 1, to obtain the Cartesian x, y, and z values.
[57/S2/F12] A perspective projection is computed by multiplying a point vector in the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system by a perspective projection matrix that is more complex than the parallel projection matrix, in which D is the distance from the clipping window to the center of projection, which is typically assumed to be at the origin but not always; the obtained homogeneous coordinate values are then divided by the homogeneous factor w to obtain Cartesian coordinates.
[57/S2/F13] To project a point P from the world coordinate system to the two-dimensional (2D) view plane, P is first transformed to the three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system, then the view volume containing P is transformed to a canonical view volume before clipping, and finally the point in the canonical view volume is projected onto the view plane; the final point P' is obtained by multiplying P by the world coordinate system to three-dimensional (3D) view coordinate system transformation matrix, the canonical view volume transformation matrix, and the projection transformation matrix.
[57/S3/F0] A window is the same as a normalized clipping window, is another term for the view plane, and is an abstract intermediate representation that has nothing to do with the display.
[57/S3/F1] A viewport is not defined on the view plane but in device space as a portion of the final screen where the scene will be rendered, and it is defined with respect to the screen or display origin and the screen or display dimensions.
[57/S3/F2] The window-to-viewport transformation maps a point from the abstract window representation to a device-specific viewport representation, specifically from a window point (Wx, Wy) to a viewport point (Vx, Vy).
[57/S3/F3] In the window-to-viewport transformation, the transformation matrix is a three by three matrix whose Sx is defined in terms of the viewport and window dimensions along the x-axis, whose Sy is similarly defined with respect to the viewport and window dimensions along the y-axis, and whose Tx and Ty are computed in terms of Sx and Sy and the corresponding x-axis or y-axis window and viewport dimensions.
[57/S3/F4] To obtain the transformed viewport point, the window point vector is multiplied by the window-to-viewport transformation matrix, and because the resulting point is in homogeneous coordinate representation, the Cartesian point is obtained by dividing the result by the homogeneous factor W.
[57/S4/F0] Clipping defines a view volume, discards objects or portions of objects outside that view volume, and is performed before projection so that projection is applied to fewer vertices and computational complexity is reduced.
[57/S4/F1] Hidden surface removal identifies and removes objects, surfaces, or surface points inside the view volume that are not visible from a particular viewer position because they are occluded, before the scene is rendered, to reduce computational complexity and support realistic image generation.
[57/S5/F0] The fifth stage of the graphics pipeline is scan conversion.
