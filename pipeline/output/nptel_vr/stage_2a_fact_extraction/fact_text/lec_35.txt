[35/S0/F0] The requirement gathering stage in the interactive system development life cycle concerns end-user requirements rather than client requirements, and it is aimed at ensuring usability.
[35/S0/F1] Requirement specification is required for system implementation.
[35/S1/F0] System-centered design (SCD) is a design approach in which the main concern is the system rather than the user, and development is driven by the developer's skill and interest, by what can be easily built on the platform, what can be created from available tools, and what the programmer finds interesting, rather than by the user's needs and expectations.
[35/S1/F1] User-centered design (UCD), also called user-centric or human-centric design, is a design approach in which the design is based on the user's abilities, context, goals, needs, and expectations, and is ideally driven from the users' point of view rather than by the programmer's convenience.
[35/S2/F0] In requirement gathering for the user-centered calendar application, a requirement specifically refers to an end-user requirement.
[35/S2/F1] In requirement gathering for the user-centered calendar application, decisions to develop the application for a mobile platform and to use a programming language such as Java or JavaScript are related to the feasibility stage rather than to end-user usability requirements.
[35/S2/F2] In requirement gathering for the user-centered calendar application, deciding how to store information such as reminders and special events, including whether to use a relational database management system (RDBMS) tool such as MySQL, is a code-design decision, not an interface-design decision or an end-user usability requirement.
[35/S2/F3] In requirement gathering for the user-centered calendar application, some developer questions are not necessarily requirements but are part of the design stage, which may involve the interface design or the code design.
[35/S2/F4] The objective of usability requirement gathering for the user-centered calendar application is to identify end-user requirements that ensure usability and to specify them so they can be converted into plans for system design, implementation, and deployment.
[35/S3/F0] Requirements are divided into functional requirements and non-functional requirements.
[35/S3/F1] Functional requirements are the system features and abilities to carry out tasks, expressed as functions treated as black boxes that produce an output from an input once implemented.
[35/S3/F2] Non-functional requirements are system characteristics that cannot be expressed as functions and are typically related to quality attributes of software.
[35/S3/F3] Usability requirements are a type of non-functional requirement that concerns the design and qualities of the user interface that make the interface usable.
[35/S3/F4] Categories of non-functional requirements include performance-related requirements, operating constraints, economic considerations, lifecycle requirements, and interface issues.
[35/S3/F5] Interface issues refer to the design of an interface or the qualities of the interface that make the interface usable, and they concern how the system interfaces with its environment, users, and other systems.
[35/S3/F6] Interface issues include an external system interface, meaning interfacing between hardware and software, and a user interface, where usability issues arise.
[35/S3/F7] Usability is important for interactive systems intended to be used by non-experts or users who are unaware of how the system works.
[35/S4/F0] The first step in gathering end-user requirements is to specify or categorize the users of the system, because end-user requirements cannot be gathered without specified users.
[35/S4/F1] The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) definition of usability requires a usable product to have a specified context of use.
[35/S4/F2] Knowing how users behave in a specific work context is essential to ensure that the software, product, or system being built is usable, because usability is defined with respect to that specific work context.
[35/S4/F3] Contextual inquiry is one of many methods for gathering end-user requirements, which in the context of usability requirement gathering are treated as usability requirements.
[35/S4/F4] Contextual inquiry is a method more than 30 years old, whose importance in user-centered design was highlighted in 1988 under the term phenomenological research method, and by the end of the 1990s it had become well developed and popular.
[35/S4/F5] Contextual inquiry is often referred to as a semi-structured interview process that consists of both interviews and observations.
[35/S4/F6] The primary objective of contextual inquiry is to watch and observe users performing tasks in their natural work setting, that is, their work context, in order to gather more insight about user behavior and end-user requirements.
[35/S4/F7] The interview part in contextual inquiry may involve asking users questions during observations to clarify certain behaviors or activities.
[35/S4/F8] A well-planned contextual inquiry process generally takes one to two hours.
[35/S4/F9] Observations and interaction with users in contextual inquiry enable designers to learn about the task domain, work culture, and physical and social constraints at the workplace, which supports design decisions.
[35/S5/F0] Contextual inquiry is important in system development because it studies users, such as managerial staff, in their work setting, reveals their unique behavioral traits, and enables design refinement; without it, designers may create invalid designs and give essential user activities equal importance to unnecessary features, compromising usability.
[35/S5/F1] User activities observed in contextual inquiry must be supported as features in the system design; in the calendar app for managerial staff, the observed activities are setting meeting dates and reminders, so the app must support them to be usable for that user group.
