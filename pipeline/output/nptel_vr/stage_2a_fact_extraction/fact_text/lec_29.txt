[29/S1/F0] Sound is vibration in a medium, usually air but also water, other gases, liquids, or solids, and it requires a medium to propagate, so there is no sound in a vacuum unlike light, which can propagate without a medium or through a vacuum.
[29/S1/F1] Sound propagation through a medium is caused by pressure variations created when sound is generated and molecules in the medium are displaced, and these pressure variations range from a compression extreme, where pressure is extremely high, to a decompression extreme, also called a rarefaction extreme, where pressure is very low.
[29/S1/F2] Pressure variation at the human eardrum is converted into a perceptual auditory experience, because pressure at the human eardrum varies as a function of time.
[29/S1/F3] Sound can be represented as a sound wave, which is a plot of pressure variation against time with time on the x-axis and air pressure on the y-axis, and the pressure varies from one extreme to another like a sinusoidal wave as time progresses.
[29/S1/F4] Sound waves are primarily characterized by three properties relevant for dealing with sound waves: frequency, amplitude, and phase, because sound can be represented as a wave.
[29/S1/F5] Frequency of a sound wave is the number of alterations between one maximum and another maximum per second, measured in hertz (Hz).
[29/S1/F6] The human audible frequency range, which is a limitation of human physiology, is from 20 hertz (Hz) to 20,000 hertz, and frequencies inside this range are audible to a human ear while frequencies outside this range are not, although other animals can perceive a higher range outside the human range.
[29/S1/F7] Amplitude of a sound wave is the maximum displacement from the mean or rest position of the wave, measured in decibel (dB) relative to the minimum sound detectable by humans at 1000 hertz (Hz).
[29/S1/F8] Phase of a sound wave is the start position of a cycle of the wave, which can begin from 0 to 360 degrees, and is measured in degrees.
[29/S2/F0] The human hearing system contains three parts: the outer ear, the middle ear, and the inner ear.
[29/S2/F1] In the outer ear, the receiver part receives a sound wave and forces it to propagate through the crucial outer-ear canal, which works as an amplifier by boosting the sound pressure for a range of frequencies.
[29/S2/F2] The complex folds in the outer ear's receiver part and outer-ear canal attenuate high-frequency sound waves.
[29/S2/F3] In the middle ear, a sound wave passing through the outer-ear canal strikes the tympanic membrane, causing it to vibrate, and the vibration is transferred to three ossicles in an air-filled chamber: the malleus, the incus, and the stapes.
[29/S2/F4] The inner ear houses the cochlea, which is the sense organ for hearing, in a structure the size of a marble.
[29/S2/F5] The cochlea is connected to nerves or neurons and converts sound energy into neural impulses that are transmitted to the brain through the neural network.
