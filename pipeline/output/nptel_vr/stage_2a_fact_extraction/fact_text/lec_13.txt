[13/S0/F0] A complete view of a virtual reality (VR) system consists of the organism, the VR hardware, and the surrounding physical world.
[13/S0/F1] In the complete view of a virtual reality (VR) system, data flow from the organism to the VR hardware is labeled tracking, data flow from the VR hardware to the organism is labeled stimulation, and bidirectional data flows exist between the VR hardware and the surrounding physical world and between the surrounding physical world and the organism.
[13/S0/F2] In a complete view of a virtual reality (VR) system, the organism component, which typically refers to the human user, is equally important to account for as the VR hardware component and cannot be omitted because the user is always present.
[13/S1/F0] In a virtual reality (VR) system, the data flow between the hardware and the user (organism) is bidirectional: stimulation data flow carries hardware-generated stimuli that override the user's senses, and tracking data flow uses sensors to track user motions so the hardware can adjust the stimuli based on those motions.
[13/S1/F1] In a virtual reality (VR) system, head tracking is commonly used and is the most important thing to track, while tracking may also include button presses (including which button is pressed in which context), controller movements, eye movements, and movements of other body parts.
[13/S2/F0] The overall virtual reality (VR) system has three components: the user, the hardware, and the surrounding physical environment.
[13/S2/F1] The surrounding physical environment is considered part of the virtual reality (VR) system because the user may respond to real-world stimuli such as light, sound, and smell, and may not be completely detached from the real world, so the environment may affect the user's behavior while interacting with the virtual world.
[13/S2/F2] A user's physical movement while interacting with the virtual world may change the surrounding physical environment.
[13/S2/F3] Virtual reality (VR) hardware may include trackers that track objects in the surrounding physical environment rather than the user directly, and this is required especially when interaction with those objects is part of the experience.
[13/S2/F4] Virtual reality (VR) hardware may change the surrounding physical environment, for example when an immersive application uses a robotic arm to change the position of objects in the environment, and that change may affect the user's behavior.
[13/S2/F5] Virtual reality (VR) hardware is not limited to only the computer, head-mounted display (HMD), or controller used by the user; it may deal with objects in the surrounding physical environment.
[13/S3/F0] Virtual reality (VR) hardware has three major components: displays (output devices), sensors (input devices), and computers (processing devices).
[13/S3/F1] In virtual reality (VR) hardware, a display or output device is used in a broad sense to mean any device that produces output that stimulates the user's sensory organs, not only a visual display.
[13/S3/F2] In virtual reality (VR) hardware, a sensor or input device is used in a broad sense to mean any tracking device that tracks the user's input and extracts information from the real world, with the discussion restricted to sensors that track the user's movements.
[13/S3/F3] In virtual reality (VR) hardware, a computer or processing device is used in a broad sense to mean a device that processes inputs and produces outputs for the user to perceive the world.
[13/S4/F0] Virtual reality (VR) input devices are broadly divided into hand input devices, which require hands to operate, and non-hand input devices, which do not require hands to operate.
[13/S4/F1] Hand input devices in virtual reality (VR) fall into five broad categories: world-grounded devices, non-tracked handheld controllers, tracked handheld controllers, hand-worn devices, and bare-hand inputs.
[13/S4/F2] World-grounded hand input devices are devices that are fixed in the real world and do not move; a joystick is an example that is commonly used to interact with desktop or other grounded systems, and portability is an issue because they constrain the user to move within a specified area.
[13/S4/F3] Non-tracked handheld controllers are hand input devices that are held in the hand but are not tracked in three-dimensional (3D) space, so the system does not know their exact position with respect to a reference frame; examples include buttons, joysticks, triggers, and traditional video game controllers.
[13/S4/F4] Non-tracked handheld controllers can cause break-in presence because the user's untracked hand movements may not produce a corresponding change in the position of the virtual hands in the virtual world, creating discomfort about whether the user is performing the task appropriately and affecting the perception of presence in the virtual world.
[13/S4/F5] Tracked handheld controllers are hand input devices with six degrees of freedom tracking, so the system can track their movements; input controllers that come with modern virtual reality (VR) systems are tracked devices so that break-in presence is avoided, and VIVE controllers are an example because they combine a joystick with tracking and are mainly used in VR.
[13/S4/F6] Tracked handheld controllers have the limitation that users cannot directly or fully touch and feel passive virtual objects through tangible interaction because the user's hands are occupied with the controller, although force feedback controllers may sometimes resolve the issue.
[13/S4/F7] Hand-worn hand input devices are devices that are worn on the hands, arms, or fingers; examples include data gloves, electromyography (EMG) bands, and rings, with data gloves being more popular.
[13/S4/F8] Bend-sensing data gloves are data gloves with sensors at all bend positions or joints and other positions; their processing unit receives continuous raw data from these bend sensors and processes it to detect hand postures and gestures, and because their many sensors make any gesture likely to generate a different data stream for unambiguous interpretation, they provide a large degree of freedom that can make interaction more natural than controller-based interaction.
[13/S4/F9] Pinch gloves are data gloves in which the sensors are placed only at fingertip positions, which may vary but often include the thumb and index finger fingertips; they detect a limited set of pinch gestures, are good at determining whether the fingertips are touching, and are typically used for discrete input such as object selection and mode switching.
[13/S4/F10] The advantages of hand-worn hand input devices are natural input with potentially rich gesture interaction, comfortable hand positions, no line-of-sight issue unlike some controller-based input, and the ability of the user's hands and fingers to fully interact with real objects, making different sorts of perception possible.
[13/S4/F11] Issues with hand-worn hand input devices are that recognizing many gestures requires users to recalibrate often or to learn and remember many gestures, which can create problems for different user categories, and that long exposure time can become uncomfortable because the hands may become sweaty.
[13/S4/F12] Bare-hand inputs are the most natural hand input interactions: the system tracks the user's bare hand using computer vision techniques, the user does not need to wear anything or use any external device, and computer vision algorithms detect the user's gestures and translate them into commands in the virtual environment, leaving the user's hands completely free.
[13/S4/F13] The Leap Motion device is an example of a bare-hand input device: it is an infrared (IR)-based hand-tracking sensor that typically contains three IR light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and two wide-angle cameras; the LEDs generate IR light reflected from the hands and picked up by the cameras, and software converts the signals into gestures.
[13/S4/F14] The Leap Motion device can be attached to a head-mounted display (HMD) to provide hand input support even if the HMD does not support hand input, and it is typically useful with low-cost HMDs that do not provide good-quality controllers or controller support, such as cardboard-type devices in which a smartphone provides a stereoscopic view and which do not support interaction.
[13/S4/F15] The Leap Motion device has problems: fatigue may occur from holding hands in front of its sensor or camera, the hand must be in the line of sight of its camera, and consistent recognition of gestures across a wide range of users may be an issue.
[13/S5/F0] Non-hand-based input devices are input devices that capture input from parts of the body other than the hand, including head movement, eye gaze, voice, and body movement.
[13/S5/F1] Head-tracking-based input devices are non-hand-based input devices that typically use head motion for input, and a head-mounted display (HMD) worn by the user can double as a head tracking device with sensors that detect yaw, roll, and pitch and infer the input to change the view.
[13/S5/F2] Eye tracking devices are non-hand-based input devices that typically come integrated with headsets and track eye movements, including where the user is looking and how the eyes are moving, helping the system understand the user's eye movement and infer what action to take.
[13/S5/F3] The Midas touch problem in eye tracking-based input is the inability to differentiate an intentional gaze meant to select an object from a habitual blank gaze, so the system may select an object or perform an action even though the user did not intend to.
[13/S5/F4] Multimodal input, which uses other forms of input in addition to eye tracking, is generally preferable over only eye tracking-based input for interacting with a system because eye tracking alone can cause objects to be selected or actions to be performed from unintentional gazes.
[13/S6/F0] Virtual reality (VR) hardware must be understood from the overall system point of view, considering the multiple components of the system, not only the hardware part, and how those components interact with the hardware, so that the requirements and the hardware to look for are known.
[13/S6/F1] The three major hardware components of any virtual reality (VR) system are input, output, and processor.
