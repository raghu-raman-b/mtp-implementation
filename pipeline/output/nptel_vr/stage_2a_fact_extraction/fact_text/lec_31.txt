[31/S0/F0] The human somatosensory system is a sensory system, like the human visual and auditory perceptual systems, that handles touch, proprioception, and kinesthesis.
[31/S0/F1] Proprioception, called the sixth sense, is the conscious awareness of the pose and movement of each movable human body part, such as legs, arms, fingers, tongue, mouth, and lips, relative to other body parts.
[31/S0/F2] Kinesthesis is the awareness of the parameters associated with moving body parts, and it provides information on velocities, accelerations, and forces during movement.
[31/S0/F3] The human somatosensory system has at least nine major kinds of receptors, of which six are fully devoted to the sense of touch and the remaining three are devoted to proprioception and kinesthesis.
[31/S0/F4] Touch receptors are embedded in the epidermis and dermis, which are the first and second layers of the skin, and the skin also has a hypodermis layer.
[31/S0/F5] The touch receptors of the human somatosensory system include at least Merkel's discs, hair follicle receptors, free nerve endings, Ruffini's endings, Meissner's corpuscles, and Pacinian corpuscles.
[31/S0/F6] The receptors used for proprioception and kinesthesis in the human somatosensory system include muscle spindles embedded in muscles that report changes in muscle length to the brain, Golgi tendon organs embedded in tendons that connect muscles to bones and report changes in muscle tension associated with movement, and joint receptors at joints between bones that coordinate muscle movement and provide information on relative bone positions.
[31/S0/F7] When a hand touches an object, activated somatosensory receptors transform the input into neural impulses that are transmitted through a neural network from neuron to neuron to the brain, and the brain processes these impulses into touch perception and can send messages back through the same pathway to cause corrective motor actions, such as removing the hand from a hot object, and the same process is equally applicable to the proprioceptive sense.
[31/S1/F0] Haptic perception is a major part of somatosensory perception, and it is the perception of the overall geometric shape of objects as part of touch perception.
[31/S1/F1] Haptic exploration is the handling or manipulation of geometric objects that is used to infer the overall geometric shape of the object.
[31/S1/F2] Haptic perception can be thought of at different levels: receptors in the skin receive input from the real world and process the information to perceive something through touch, while higher-level perception requires object manipulations that are called haptic explorations.
[31/S1/F3] The six types of haptic exploration, each needed for a different object property perception, are lateral motion for texture perception, pressure for hardness perception, enclosure for global shape or volume perception, static contact for temperature perception, unsupported holding for weight perception, and contour following for local shape perception.
[31/S1/F4] Each haptic exploration used to perceive different properties of objects involves different kinds of receptors and a combination of spatial and temporal information.
[31/S1/F5] The overall goal of haptic exploration is to integrate somatosensory signals from in-hand manipulation of an object so that a geometric mental model of the object is learned in terms of properties such as shape, size, weight and hardness.
[31/S1/F6] Haptic perception is important for creating a mental model of the surrounding world, and because mental model creation is an essential part of an immersive experience, haptic perception can improve an immersive experience.
[31/S1/F7] Haptic perception and haptic exploration offer an extra dimension to virtual reality (VR) by letting users feel the virtual environment through the sense of touch, which gives users a better feeling of the virtual world and helps them feel truly immersed in the virtual world, and because immersion in the real world also involves haptic exploration, adding haptic exploration to a virtual world increases the immersive experience.
[31/S1/F8] In a virtual desert walking example, visual and auditory perception alone may not give a complete picture of the haptic touch perception of feeling sand glide under the feet, and adding haptic exploration support through proper hardware and software makes the experience far better than an experience using only visual or auditory senses.
[31/S2/F0] Touch sensation in extended reality (XR) can be provided by engineered devices, and the most popular form is force feedback, typically in the form of vibrations, provided by force feedback devices such as a force feedback mouse, smart gloves, and body suits, which are common in many XR systems, particularly virtual reality (VR) systems.
[31/S2/F1] The KGS dot view model DV-2 is a haptic pin array with a matrix of pins that a computer controls, and the pins are forced upward, according to signals generated for objects in the virtual world, to simulate the texture of an object surface when the user's fingertip scans across it.
[31/S2/F2] Controlling a haptic pin array precisely enough to give a realistic feeling of a textured surface is generally difficult and challenging.
[31/S2/F3] Real-world objects whose virtual avatars appear in a virtual world and that are used in place of computer-controlled haptic devices to provide feedback to the user's somatosensory system are sometimes called tangible user interfaces.
[31/S2/F4] To give the feeling of touching a real object surface in a virtual world, using real objects to provide touch feedback is generally considered less challenging than creating a computer-controlled haptic device.
[31/S2/F5] The basic idea for implementing tangible user interfaces is to create the experience by aligning the real world and the virtual world.
[31/S2/F6] A see-through display such as Microsoft HoloLens, which is typically good for mixed reality or augmented reality, enables users to see and interact with the physical world around them, and it adds virtual objects to the real world or visually enhances real objects.
[31/S2/F7] In a see-through display such as Microsoft HoloLens, the computer uses wearable device sensors to detect which real object the user is dealing with and can change the view of that object or add virtual objects to the real world.
[31/S3/F0] Taste and smell are generally grouped as chemical senses because their chemoreceptors work by chemical reaction with specific molecules or chemical stimuli at appropriate concentrations, and not all chemoreceptors respond to all chemical stimuli.
[31/S3/F1] There is much less research on integrating smell and taste into extended reality (XR) systems than on visual, auditory, and touch senses, and fewer electronic devices have been made or are being made to provide stimuli to the nose and tongue, the body organs responsible for smell and taste.
[31/S3/F2] Adding scent or smell to films is an old idea that can be traced back to the early 20th century, and historical systems that included smells include Smell-O-Vision, developed in 1960, which injected 30 different odors into movie theater seats at different points during a film according to the scenery, and Sensorama.
[31/S3/F3] A smell interface, also called an olfactory display, provides odor stimuli to the nose, usually by using air pumps or micropumps to spray chemical compounds near the nose, with other components that help create the odor.
[31/S3/F4] For smell interfaces, delivering odor near the user's nose for a personal experience is relatively easier than delivering it on a room scale for social or larger experiences, because room-scale delivery generally makes it hard to control odor intensity and uniformity and to vary odor concentration over a large area for localization, which is not easy with current research.
[31/S3/F5] When olfactory displays can generate odor effects, they are shown to induce strong cognitive and emotional responses that make an immersive experience more attractive and more immersive, and pleasant odors can help reduce visually induced motion sickness, also called cybersickness, to some extent.
[31/S3/F6] A taste interface can place electrodes over and under the tongue to provide stimulation that simulates the main taste types.
[31/S3/F7] It is generally difficult to develop taste interfaces without causing people to eat food during the experience, which is not a convenient arrangement and is generally quite difficult to achieve in practice.
