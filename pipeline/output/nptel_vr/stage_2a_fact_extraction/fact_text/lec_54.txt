[54/S0/F0] Extended reality (XR), in particular virtual reality (VR), is resource hungry, meaning that XR and VR require a lot of resources.
[54/S0/F1] The objectives of virtual reality (VR) resource optimization are to minimize resource requirements without compromising the VR experience, that is, to maximize the VR experience.
[54/S0/F2] In extended reality (XR) and particularly in virtual reality (VR), resources can include any or all of hardware, software, manpower, and supporting infrastructure; in VR these include hardware such as input devices, processing devices, and output devices; software such as development tools, software development kits, software packages, or application packages; manpower such as staff for development, deployment, maintenance, and marketing; and supporting infrastructure such as rooms to store the hardware setup, electricity supply, furniture, and network infrastructure such as network gear, switches, routers, wires, and servers.
[54/S0/F3] Each resource type in extended reality (XR) can be optimized separately, but such optimization is not easy because the considerations vary widely and include money such as availability of funds and how resources are managed, availability of the resources before use, policy issues, and geopolitical issues.
[54/S0/F4] Virtual reality (VR) resource optimization generally does not try to optimize every resource, assumes some resources are given, and instead focuses on hardware requirements at the client end (the user end), because focusing on client-end hardware is the most straightforward way to optimize resources without being entangled in many issues; the objective is for the end user to have a good experience with affordable hardware resources.
[54/S1/F0] For a good virtual reality (VR) immersive experience, client-end hardware is needed to support requirements arising from rendering or computer graphics and to manage rendering or computer graphics related issues, and the complexity required to render a scene corresponds to the need for high-end hardware support.
[54/S1/F1] The image displayed on a computer screen is the whole interface, that is the content seen on the screen, not only the main object, and it comprises geometric shapes or objects, including interface objects such as menus and icons, with colors assigned to them.
[54/S1/F2] In computer graphics, interaction is the manipulation of an image or parts of it by a user using input devices, such as a joystick or devices associated with virtual reality (VR) input, which changes the object's appearance and the screen.
[54/S1/F3] Computers understand only binary representation, that is zeros and ones, whereas humans perceive realistic representation, not strings of zeros and ones.
[54/S1/F4] The fundamental problem of computer graphics is to create or represent objects, synthesize objects into a scene, and render the scene on a computer display so that humans can perceive it.
[54/S1/F5] The fundamental problem of computer graphics gives rise to four basic issues: representing objects or parts of an image; synthesizing parts to form complete realistic imagery; allowing users to manipulate images on a screen, which is related to interactive systems; and creating the perception of motion, which is related to animation.
[54/S2/F0] A generic graphics system has a host computer and a display, with three major components in between: a display controller, video memory, and a video controller.
[54/S2/F1] In a generic graphics system, the host computer, or its central processing unit (CPU), has two-way communication with the display controller, in which the CPU sends display commands and may receive interaction data.
[54/S2/F2] The display controller is responsible for image generation, meaning it creates a representation of a scene as a two-dimensional image that can be displayed on a two-dimensional screen.
[54/S2/F3] Image generation is a multi-stage computational process, and if all rendering-related computations were carried out by the central processing unit (CPU), the CPU's time would be consumed by rendering, leaving it with very little time for other tasks and the system could do little except rendering; to avoid this and increase efficiency, rendering is usually carried out by a dedicated component.
[54/S2/F4] The dedicated rendering component is typically the graphics card, which usually contains its own graphics processing unit (GPU); in a generic graphics system this component is called the display controller, and the central processing unit (CPU) passes graphics rendering tasks to it so the computation takes place in the GPU rather than in the CPU.
[54/S2/F5] The display controller generates the image in a digital format, as strings of zeros and ones, and sends that image representation to video memory for storage.
[54/S2/F6] Video memory is the dedicated part of the overall memory hierarchy of the computer where the digital image is stored, typically as part of a separate graphics unit, and in modern graphics cards it is sometimes called video random-access memory (VRAM).
[54/S2/F7] A graphic display screen generally has a grid of picture elements, or pixels; when these pixels are activated, or excited, by an electrical or electromechanical means, they emit light with specific intensities that the viewer perceives as a colored image on the screen.
[54/S2/F8] The video controller uses the data stored in video memory to render an image on the screen, converting the digital image into some analog voltages required to activate the pixels, and it controls the pixel excitation mechanism using the video memory content.
[54/S3/F0] Graphic devices are broadly of two types, determined by the method used for excitation of pixels: vector scan devices and raster scan devices.
[54/S3/F1] Vector scan devices, also called random scan, stroke writing, or calligraphic devices, view an image as composed of continuous geometric primitives such as lines and curves and render the image by exciting only selected pixels that represent the primitives, on the assumption that rendering the primitives will make the whole image be perceived.
[54/S3/F2] Vector scan devices require high precision to target and excite specific pixels, which makes vector scan devices costly and typically unaffordable for mass production or mass consumption; vector scan devices are therefore typically used in specialized settings where limited quantities are required.
[54/S3/F3] A raster scan device views an image as represented by the whole pixel grid, activates all pixels in sequence, typically left to right and top to bottom, with appropriate inputs that make the pixels emit light at appropriate intensities, and the sequential activation of all pixels is called scanning or raster scanning.
[54/S3/F4] Raster scan rendering requires less precision and is much cheaper than vector scan rendering.
[54/S3/F5] Commercial products such as smartphones, televisions, and any device with a computer and display typically comprise raster scan displays.
[54/S4/F0] In a raster scan system, the frame buffer is the video memory component that stores the intensity values for all pixels in the grid, and it contains one memory location for each pixel that stores the intensity as a bit string of 0s and 1s.
[54/S4/F1] The size of a frame buffer is the size of the screen and is equal to the screen resolution of a raster scan device.
[54/S4/F2] In a raster scan device, the light emitted from excited pixel elements decays over time, causing the rendered scene to fade even when the image is not changing.
[54/S4/F3] In raster scan rendering, pixels are excited in a left-to-right, top-to-bottom order, so the top-left pixel is excited much earlier than the bottom-right pixel; because the emitted light decays, pixels may fade out of sync and the displayed image may appear non-uniform or distorted.
[54/S4/F4] Screen refreshing is the periodic re-excitation of pixels on a raster scan device, done to avoid fading and distortion and to keep the rendered image uniform even when the image is not changing.
[54/S4/F5] The refresh rate is the number of times a scene is refreshed per second on a screen, is represented in hertz, and a good refresh rate for most devices is 60 hertz.
[54/S4/F6] A higher refresh rate is always preferable for a virtual reality (VR) experience because the VR screen is much closer to the eyes than in typical computer usage, where the screen and the eyes are at a reasonable distance.
[54/S4/F7] In a color display, each pixel contains multiple light-emitting elements, typically three although sometimes four can be used for different purposes; the typical three elements emit red, green, and blue primary colors at different intensities.
[54/S4/F8] Color is perceived when the red, green, and blue light emitted by the pixel elements of a color display is mixed in the eye, so the observed color is a combination of different amounts of red, green, and blue.
[54/S5/F0] The three-dimensional (3D) computer graphics pipeline (3D graphics pipeline) is a stage-wise process of computation starting from object representation to rendering on the pixel grid.
