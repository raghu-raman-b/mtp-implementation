[27/S0/F0] In virtual reality (VR) headsets, light emitted from the displays reaches the retina and is guided by engineered lenses in the headset and natural lenses in the eye, a combined system that helps the user perceive immersive visual content.
[27/S0/F1] Knowledge of how light propagates through lenses and the human eye is important for understanding how virtual reality (VR) headsets work and how they lead to visual perception.
[27/S0/F2] Knowledge of the physics of light propagation is used to understand the construction of virtual worlds by modeling light propagation and determining what should be rendered on visual displays.
[27/S0/F3] Knowledge of how light propagates, how lenses and the eye work, and how cameras work is used to understand camera setups, choose camera positions in virtual worlds for the best experience, and present virtual worlds through panoramic videos.
[27/S0/F4] Understanding how a camera works helps build tracking hardware and software.
[27/S1/F0] Light can be described by three views—the photon (particle) view, the wave view, and the ray view—and these views can be used to explain different phenomena related to light.
[27/S1/F1] The photon (particle) view of light describes light as a large number of photons, tiny energy particles that move through space at a very high speed, often called the speed of light, and it is helpful for understanding or calculating the amount of light received by a sensor or receptor.
[27/S1/F2] The wave view of light describes light as waves, ripples through space, and it is helpful for explaining the spectrum of color rather than explaining color in terms of light as particles.
[27/S1/F3] The ray view of light describes light as rays, which are trajectories or paths assumed to be traces of the motion of a single hypothetical photon, and it is helpful for explaining how lenses work and the concept of visibility.
[27/S2/F0] When light rays strike a material surface, the possible interactions are transmission, absorption, and reflection, and either one of these interactions or all three can occur.
[27/S2/F1] In light transmission, some of the light rays that strike a material surface pass through the material and come out of the material.
[27/S2/F2] In absorption, some of the light rays that strike a material surface are absorbed by the material, terminate inside the material, and do not propagate further outside the material.
[27/S2/F3] In reflection, a light ray strikes a material surface and is reflected from the surface without entering or penetrating the material.
[27/S3/F0] In color perception, the perceived color of a surface point is determined by the intensity of the light reaching the viewer's eye.
[27/S3/F1] Ambient light is light in the environment, reflected from surrounding objects, that reaches the viewer's eye together with light reflected from the object's surface.
[27/S4/F0] Light incident on the human eye passes in the order cornea, pupil, and lens, and through internal semi-liquid media such as the aqueous humor between the cornea and the pupil and the vitreous humor between the lens and the retina, before reaching the retina at the back of the eye.
[27/S4/F1] The material that makes up the cornea and the lens of the human eye refracts light so that images are focused on the retina.
[27/S4/F2] The iris controls the amount of light entering the human eye by dilating or constricting the pupil, typically dilating it in dark surroundings to admit more light and constricting it in bright light to admit less.
[27/S4/F3] The retina at the back of the eye consists of photoreceptors, which are the bodily sensors required for visual perception, and optic nerve fibers, which are neurons that carry the signal to the brain.
[27/S4/F4] The human eye contains two types of photoreceptors, rods and cones, in which rods are more numerous in the peripheral region of the retina and cones are mainly located in the small central region of the retina called the fovea.
[27/S4/F5] Multiple rods can share a single optic nerve fiber, which aids sensitivity to lower levels of light, whereas approximately one optic nerve fiber serves each cone, which aids image acuity.
[27/S4/F6] Vision accomplished only with cones is photopic vision, which is more related to direct or central vision, and vision accomplished only with rods is scotopic vision, which is more related to peripheral vision.
[27/S4/F7] Color is perceived only in photopic vision, which is accomplished with cones and gives a sense of color, whereas in scotopic vision weak lights are perceived as a series of grays and color perception is not that good.
[27/S5/F0] Visible light is a spectrum of frequencies of electromagnetic light waves, ranging from red light at 4.3 times 10 to the power 14 hertz and 700 nanometer wavelength to violet light at 7.5 times 10 to the power 14 hertz and 400 nanometer wavelength.
[27/S5/F1] Visible light waves can excite cones in the eye, producing photopic vision, which is color perception, while light waves outside the visible light spectrum cannot excite cones and therefore do not produce color perception.
[27/S6/F0] Human eyes support two types of vision, central vision and peripheral vision, because photoreceptor density across the retina varies greatly.
[27/S6/F1] The fovea is the central region of the retina with the greatest concentration of photoreceptors, particularly cones.
[27/S6/F2] The innermost part of the fovea has a diameter of only 0.5 millimeters or an angular range of 0.85 plus or minus 0.85 degrees and contains almost entirely cones.
[27/S6/F3] Central vision requires the eyes to be focused and pointed straight at an object to perceive a sharp color image.
[27/S6/F4] Peripheral vision is supported by the peripheral region of the retina, where rays entering the cornea from the sides land, rod density is lower and cone density is very low, and photoreceptor density is lower, with a mix of rods and an even lesser amount of cones.
[27/S6/F5] Peripheral vision is better than central vision at detecting movement, but it does not provide effective color perception.
[27/S6/F6] The blind spot is a region of the retina with no photoreceptors, caused by the retina's inside-out anatomy, which leaves no other way to route neural signals to the brain, and by the optic nerve spreading out around the retina, so light or visual stimuli falling there produce no perception.
