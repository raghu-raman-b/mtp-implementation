[18/S0/F0] Tracking technologies used in virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) are categorized into electromechanical, electromagnetic, acoustic or ultrasonic, vision-based, inertial, and others, where the others category includes location-based and hybrid tracking technologies.
[18/S1/F0] Electromechanical trackers consist of a serial or parallel kinematic structure composed of links that are interconnected using joints with sensors.
[18/S1/F1] Electromechanical trackers are of two types: world-fixed trackers and wearable trackers.
[18/S1/F2] A world-fixed electromechanical tracker is a setup that is quite cumbersome, with a display attached to an electromechanically tracked setup that has mechanical pivoting, tracking sensors, and a counterbalance to make it stable.
[18/S1/F3] Wearable electromechanical trackers are worn on a person's body and track and translate the movements of that person into animated avatar movement in a virtual world.
[18/S1/F4] Wearable electromechanical trackers include products such as IGS 190, Gypsy 6, Gypsy Gyro, and exoskeleton tracker.
[18/S1/F5] Wearable electromechanical trackers are not very comfortable to wear, are quite cumbersome, and are used in limited, highly specialized setups.
[18/S2/F0] Electromagnetic trackers are non-contact position measurement devices in which a stationary transmitter in the environment produces a magnetic field, and a receiver attached to the headset moves with the head, and the receiver's real-time position is obtained from the magnetic field direction.
[18/S2/F1] In an electromagnetic tracker, the transmitter generally has three antennas that produce three orthogonal magnetic fields, and these fields generate alternating current (AC) or direct current (DC) in the receiver; the resulting currents help track the head's position relative to a fixed position in the environment.
[18/S2/F2] Acoustic tracking, also called ultrasonic tracking, uses ultrasonic signals from stationary transmitters, which can be called speakers, to determine the real-time position of receivers, which can be called microphones, that are attached to a head-mounted display (HMD), so the head's position or orientation can be tracked.
[18/S2/F3] Electromagnetic tracking and acoustic or ultrasonic tracking both use stationary transmitters and receivers that move with the head or the user's body.
[18/S2/F4] Acoustic or ultrasonic tracking requires line of sight between the transmitter and receiver, with no obstacle in between, whereas electromagnetic tracking does not require this line of sight.
[18/S3/F0] Vision-based trackers are more prevalent trackers that determine poses, such as position and orientation, using optical sensors or cameras.
[18/S3/F1] The three main categories of optical sensors that can be deployed to detect or track a pose are infrared sensors, visible light sensors, and three-dimensional (3D) structure sensors.
[18/S3/F2] Infrared tracking is considered to be among the earliest vision-based tracking techniques developed and used in virtual reality (VR) or augmented reality (AR).
[18/S3/F3] Infrared tracking uses targets that emit or reflect light, which makes detection easy due to high brightness compared with the surroundings.
[18/S3/F4] Infrared tracking is robust to adverse illumination effects such as poor ambient lighting or harsh shadows because it relies on infrared sensing rather than visible light sensing.
[18/S3/F5] Infrared tracking can be done in two ways: outside looking in and inside looking out.
[18/S3/F6] In outside looking in infrared tracking, cameras placed in the environment rather than on the tracked object detect targets attached to the object and use them to determine the object's pose and position.
[18/S3/F7] In inside looking out infrared tracking, the camera is mounted with the user and detects external targets, from which it estimates poses.
[18/S4/F0] Visible light tracking uses visible light sensors or optical cameras; optical cameras are the most common optical sensor type and are available in almost all consumer-grade electronic devices, including laptops, smartphones, and tablet personal computers (PCs), and in wearable devices, giving an abundant supply of such sensors.
[18/S4/F1] Visible light tracking is classified into three tracking techniques: fiducial tracking, natural feature tracking, and model-based tracking.
[18/S4/F2] Fiducial tracking adds fiducials, which are artificial landmarks in the environment, to aid registration and tracking of the object being tracked.
[18/S4/F3] The complexity of fiducial tracking depends on the kind of fiducial used, such as small colored light-emitting diodes (LEDs) or pieces of paper, multi-ring fiducials for detection at greater distances, proportional width ring fiducials whose appearance changes with distance, or constant width ring fiducials whose ring width remains constant.
[18/S5/F0] Natural feature tracking is the second category of visual light-based sensing that registers camera pose in real time using features already present in the natural environment, and it requires advanced image processing or computer vision techniques.
[18/S5/F1] In natural feature tracking, the advanced image processing algorithms or computer vision techniques detect features that are unique to the surroundings with respect to the object to be tracked, such as points, corners, and line intersections.
[18/S5/F2] In natural feature tracking, unique descriptors are calculated for each detected feature, and their role is to identify and differentiate one feature from other features.
[18/S5/F3] In natural feature tracking, camera pose is estimated by using the calculated feature descriptors to match features detected in the scene with features detected in the object to be tracked.
[18/S5/F4] Popular natural feature detection and description algorithms used for natural feature tracking include scale invariant feature transform (SIFT), speeded up robust features (SURF), binary robust independent elementary features (BRIEF), and binary robust invariant scalable key points (BRISK).
[18/S6/F0] Model-based tracking is the third category of visible light-based sensing.
[18/S6/F1] Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is a technique that simultaneously creates and updates a map of the real environment while localizing positions within it, and as time passes the map is updated with the object's changing position, enabling real-time tracking.
[18/S6/F2] Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) was originally developed for robot navigation and subsequently adapted for augmented reality.
[18/S6/F3] Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) allows drift-free tracking of unknown environments.
[18/S6/F4] Parallel Tracking and Mapping (PTAM) is a further optimization of Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) that allows tracking of the camera and mapping of the environment as separate activities.
[18/S6/F5] Parallel Tracking and Mapping (PTAM) was specifically designed for augmented reality.
[18/S6/F6] Parallel Tracking and Mapping (PTAM) improved both the accuracy of tracking and the overall performance.
[18/S6/F7] Parallel Tracking and Mapping (PTAM) is more suitable for smaller environments because the computational requirements for larger environments are quite high.
[18/S7/F0] 3D structure tracking (3D structure sensing) is a recent tracking technique that uses commercial sensors to obtain the 3D positions of points in the scene and creates 3D models of the structures in the environment for augmentation of the real-world view.
[18/S7/F1] Commercial sensors capable of detecting 3D structure information from the environment became affordable in recent years, a trend that started with the introduction of the Microsoft Kinect device, and similar devices are available.
[18/S7/F2] Kinect Fusion, an augmented reality (AR) system by Microsoft and an example of 3D structure tracking, uses data from the Kinect's structured light depth sensor to create high-quality 3D models of real objects and environments, and the models are also used for tracking the pose of the Kinect.
[18/S8/F0] Inertial trackers are self-contained sensors that measure rate of change in object orientation, translation velocity, or acceleration, and they measure only changes rather than absolute values.
[18/S8/F1] Inertial trackers are typically based on microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) and are part of the device that the user is using, for example most smartphones and head-mounted displays (HMDs) nowadays, so they have no range limitation and can be readily used for tracking.
[18/S8/F2] Hybrid software-level sensors can be designed using the primary inertial sensors (a gyroscope, an accelerometer, and a magnetometer) and can be part of the device.
[18/S8/F3] Basic types of tracking sensors typically used include electromechanical, electromagnetic, inertial, three-dimensional (3D) structure sensing, and light-based sensing.
[18/S8/F4] Sensing approaches that do not directly fit into the basic tracking sensor categories include acoustic sensing and the global positioning system (GPS).
[18/S8/F5] The global positioning system (GPS) is a positional tracking device that is particularly useful in outdoor environments.
[18/S8/F6] The global positioning system (GPS) generally offers low accuracy, particularly in indoor environments, and allows only positional tracking.
[18/S8/F7] Real time kinematic (RTK) technology can be used with a basic global positioning system (GPS) sensor to improve detection accuracy by using global positioning system signal carrier waves for measurement, providing further accuracy than the basic global positioning system.
[18/S8/F8] The global positioning system (GPS) is typically utilized when accurate position registration is not important, when an approximation is good enough, or as part of a hybrid tracking system with other tracking systems.
[18/S9/F0] Motion capture is a technique for digitally recording movements of real things, usually humans, and it can be based on any tracking method, so it is not a fundamentally new tracking method.
[18/S9/F1] Motion capture is packaged as wearable suits that include tracking sensors, typically using one or two facial trackers and four or five full body tracking devices that can be cameras, and it records the movements of users who wear the suits and make movements.
[18/S9/F2] Motion capture need not be real-time tracking; it can be non-real-time tracking as well.
[18/S9/F3] Eye-tracking systems can be part of other trackers and can be head-mounted or remotely attached to a display or an external display.
[18/S9/F4] Eye-tracking systems use optical tracking with a high-resolution camera-based tracking device, and they can also use techniques such as infrared lighting-based eye tracking, electro-oculography (EOG), and video-oculography (VOG).
[18/S9/F5] Eye-tracking systems typically track the gaze point or area of the gaze of the user.
[18/S9/F6] A hybrid tracker is a tracking method that uses two or more basic tracking systems or approaches, formed by combining basic tracking methods, and emotion capture is an example of a hybrid tracker.
[18/S9/F7] Typically, hybrid trackers have been found to perform better than single-technology-based tracking.
