[7/S0/F0] The historical evolution of augmented reality (AR) can be broadly divided into four phases that may sometimes overlap, unlike the phases of virtual reality: the experimental phase before the 1980s, the basic research phase from the 1980s to the mid-1990s, the tools or applications phase from the mid-1990s to 2007, and the commercial application phase from 2007 to the present.
[7/S0/F1] The phases of augmented reality (AR) evolution are characterized as follows: the experimental phase involved early experimentation that defined the concepts and showed the types of technology required for AR; the basic research phase involved research into enabling technologies such as tracking, displays, and input devices; the tools or applications phase involved developing or exploring early applications and exploratory tasks related to interaction techniques, usability of such applications, and design theory for AR; and the commercial application phase involved widespread AR availability and newer applications appearing in domains such as gaming, medicine, mobile, commerce, and marketing.
[7/S1/F0] Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull created the first truly computer-generated augmented reality (AR) prototype in 1968.
[7/S1/F1] The first Sutherland-Sproull augmented reality (AR) prototype combined a cathode ray tube (CRT)-based optical see-through head-mounted display (HMD) with a ceiling-mounted mechanical tracking system connected to a PDP-11 computer and custom graphics hardware; the tracking system registered head movements in three dimensions on the see-through display, and the mechanical tracker was later replaced by an ultrasonic tracker.
[7/S1/F2] Tom Furness at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base began research on the United States (US) Air Force super cockpit program, which was a major research program that produced technologies relevant to augmented reality (AR).
[7/S1/F3] In the super cockpit prototype, a pilot wearing a head-mounted display (HMD) in the aircraft cockpit saw computer-generated imagery superimposed over the real-world view in daylight and saw computer-generated imagery replace the real-world view at night or in bad weather.
[7/S1/F4] The early augmented reality (AR) experiments led to head-mounted augmented reality displays used in modern aircraft, to virtual reality (VR) displays such as the VIVED head-mounted display (HMD), and to input and tracking hardware.
[7/S2/F0] The basic research phase of augmented reality (AR) came after the experimentation phase and included much influential work in the 1990s that shaped the field of augmented reality.
[7/S2/F1] Tom Caudell is often credited with coining the phrase augmented reality (AR) because he published the first academic article containing the term AR in 1992.
[7/S2/F2] During the basic research phase of augmented reality (AR), research projects at the University of North Carolina included registration tracking techniques in 1993 and 1994, early research in 1995 and 1996 on see-through head-mounted displays typically used in AR, and several applications developed alongside the basic research.
[7/S2/F3] During the basic research phase of augmented reality (AR), research at Columbia University included one of the first academic papers on AR describing the KARMA system in 1993, work in 1993 on AR interaction methods, that is, how to interact with augmented digital content, and some publications on mobile AR systems in 1997.
[7/S2/F4] During the basic research phase of augmented reality (AR), research at the University of Toronto included overlaying virtual cues on stereoscopic video from 1991 to 1993 and work on AR applications from 1992 to 1993.
[7/S2/F5] Chameleon was an early handheld or mobile augmented reality (AR) system developed in 1993 that allowed a user to move a liquid-crystal display (LCD) over a piece of paper and see virtual content.
[7/S2/F6] NaviCam by Rekimoto was a handheld or mobile augmented reality (AR) system developed in 1995 that superimposed virtual tags on real objects using handheld and head-mounted displays.
[7/S2/F7] Chameleon and NaviCam were true augmented reality (AR)-type applications and precursors to the kind of AR applications seen today.
[7/S3/F0] Notable works during the mid-1990s tools or applications phase of augmented reality (AR) included research on key enabling technologies for augmented reality, such as tracking, display, and interaction.
[7/S3/F1] Work during the mid-1990s tools or applications phase of augmented reality (AR) included systems that allowed people in the same location to see and interact with shared augmented reality content.
[7/S3/F2] Medical applications developed during the mid-1990s tools or applications phase of augmented reality (AR) included visualization of laparoscopic surgery, X-ray visualization in a patient's body, and image-guided surgery.
[7/S3/F3] From the mid-1990s, wearable computing using head-mounted displays (HMDs) emerged as a related research area in augmented reality (AR), although it had existed earlier and people began paying more attention to such head-mounted displays.
[7/S3/F4] In 1997, computer-vision techniques were used to overlay augmented reality (AR) content on real-world markers, and tracking was used to register the AR content in three dimensions.
[7/S3/F5] In 1997, wearable computers with global positioning system (GPS) tracking produced outdoor augmented reality (AR) interfaces that showed information in place in the real world, and tracking was used to register the AR content in three dimensions.
[7/S3/F6] In 2002 and 2003, outdoor wearable augmented reality (AR) applications included terrestrial navigation, on-site viewing and creation of computer-aided design (CAD) models, and game playing.
[7/S3/F7] The wearable augmented reality (AR) systems being developed in 2002 and 2003 were bulky and difficult to use because of the size of their custom computer hardware, batteries, tracking sensors, and display hardware, and their setups were technically wearable computers with a headset, a computer carried in a backpack, and different input devices.
[7/S3/F8] Viewpoint tracking was one of the most significant challenges of augmented reality (AR), and it was addressed so that three-dimensional registration of content could happen accurately.
[7/S3/F9] In 1996, Rekimoto developed a simple computer-vision tracking system based on a printed matrix that evolved into the cyber code system, as part of software that could enable an augmented reality (AR) system to track a user's viewpoint.
[7/S3/F10] In 1999, Kato and Billinghurst developed the augmented reality (AR) Toolkit tracking library, which was released in 2000 as open-source software, provided real-time computer-vision tracking of a square marker, and could enable an augmented reality system to track a user's viewpoint.
[7/S3/F11] In 2004, the first mobile-phone based augmented reality (AR) application was demonstrated and the AR Toolkit was ported to mobile phones, and although early mobile phones looked different from modern-day smartphones, people still tried to use early mobile phones to develop mobile-based augmented reality applications.
[7/S4/F0] The commercial application phase of augmented reality (AR) began in October 2007 with the release of the Sony PlayStation 3-based game The Eye of Judgment, one of the first widely used consumer AR experiences, and with its launch such AR experiences started becoming very popular after AR had previously been neither very popular nor easily available.
[7/S4/F1] In 2009, augmented reality (AR) moved from research laboratories and specialist applications to widely available commercial technology.
[7/S4/F2] The main factors behind the move of augmented reality (AR) from research laboratories and specialist applications to widely available commercial technology in 2009 were Adobe Flash-based AR authoring tools that made experiencing AR through web browsers possible from 2008, smartphone-based AR because the era of smartphones had begun by then, and the use of AR in global marketing campaigns to increase sales and make products popular.
[7/S4/F3] Starting in 2017, Apple launched the augmented reality (AR) software development kit (SDK) ARKit for AR system development, and Google released ARCore for Android operating system (OS) or Android platforms; these launches further stimulated AR growth by enabling many developers to create AR applications and increasing the number of AR applications from simple to complex ones.
[7/S4/F4] The commercial application phase of augmented reality (AR) is still ongoing and is expected to continue growing, with more AR applications and tools, hardware improvements in wearable devices, mobile devices, and holographic projection-based devices, and the development of algorithms, artificial intelligence (AI), and computer vision technologies making three-dimensional (3D) tracking and registration more feasible on low-end devices.
[7/S5/F0] The 2015 article by Billinghurst et al. is a recommended source for a more detailed account of the historical evolution of augmented reality (AR).
