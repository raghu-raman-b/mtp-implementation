[8/S0/F1, 8/S1/F7, 8/S3/F0, 8/S3/F1, 8/S3/F2, 8/S3/F4, 8/S3/F5; virtual reality, immersion, presence, sensory stimuli, virtual reality system, virtual environment]
    A Virtual reality involves two major issues: the technological issue of generating suitable sensory stimuli and the psychological issue of perceiving those stimuli. Immersion corresponds to the technological side, giving an objective and quantifiable description of what a system provides, while presence is the psychological, subjective sense of being in the virtual environment. The overall goal is high presence, so a system needs high immersion to increase the potential for presence, but immersion does not ensure presence.
  Q How do the objective qualities of immersion explain the way a virtual reality system presents the artificial environment described in the definition of virtual reality?  [8/S1/F0, 8/S1/F1, 8/S1/F2, 8/S1/F3, 8/S1/F4, 8/S1/F5, 8/S1/F6, 4/S0/F2, 4/S1/F0; virtual reality, virtual reality system, artificial environment, sensory stimuli, immersion, extensive, matching, surrounding, vivid, interactivity, plot-informing]
    A A virtual reality system is an artificial or synthetic environment in which computer-generated sensory stimuli provide the experience and user actions partially determine what happens. Immersion is the objective degree to which such a system projects stimuli, and its qualities name the dimensions of that projection. Extensive and vivid describe the range and energy of the sensory modalities, surrounding makes cues panoramic, matching and interactivity connect stimuli to user movement and action, and plot-informing makes the world's behavior understandable.
  Q How does the description of immersion as blocking real-world input relate to the objective qualities used to evaluate a virtual reality system's stimulus projection?  [8/S1/F0, 8/S1/F1, 8/S1/F3, 8/S1/F4, 2/S1/F0; immersion, sensory stimuli, virtual world, extensive, surrounding, vivid, field of view, resolution, lighting, frame rate, audio bit rate]
    A Immersion can be described as blocking real-world input so that the user receives input only from the virtual world. The objective qualities describe how the system projects that virtual input. Extensive covers more sensory modalities, surrounding makes cues more panoramic through a wider field of view, and vivid increases stimulus energy through factors such as resolution, lighting, frame rate, or audio bit rate.

16/A6  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (15 facts, 47 concepts)
  Q How do pose estimation and environment tracking together support the registration of digital content in augmented reality?  [1/S2/F1, 9/S3/F5, 16/S0/F3, 16/S1/F8, 16/S1/F7; augmented reality, pose, anchor, environment tracking, digital content, physical object, three-dimensional space, field of view, real-world view, virtual reality]
    A Augmented reality is the intermediate state in which the real environment is augmented with virtual content. To register virtual content in augmented reality, the pose of the viewer relative to an anchor in the real world must be determined, because without proper pose estimation three-dimensional registration is difficult or impossible. In augmented reality, tracking also requires moving real-world objects in the field of view, and a moved physical object must be tracked in the real-world view so that digital content can be appropriately registered. Environment tracking is more important in augmented reality for this purpose, although it is also important in virtual reality.
  Q How does the position of a sensor determine the two broad ways to obtain tracking input, and why does that affect where outside-in tracking can work?  [16/S2/F1, 16/S2/F2, 16/S2/F4, 16/S2/F5; tracking, tracker position, outside-in tracking, inside-out tracking, external sensor, sensor range, head-mounted display, pose, motion]
    A Tracking is categorized based on the position of the tracker or sensor into outside-in and inside-out tracking. In outside-in tracking, the experience device does not contain the sensors; external sensors such as cameras or markers provide data to identify and track motion, pose, and orientation. Outside-in tracking works only within the sensor range where those external sensors are placed. Inside-out tracking locates cameras or other sensors within the experience device, such as a head-mounted display, so no external devices are needed and there is no such placement restriction.
  Q What different errors are reduced by calibration and drift correction in the tracking process?  [16/S3/F1, 16/S3/F2, 16/S3/F5; tracking, tracking process, calibration, drift correction, sensor error, estimation error, position, orientation]
    A The tracking process broadly consists of calibration, registration, integration, and drift correction. Calibration transforms the output of imperfect sensors so that it behaves as closely as possible to the natural or real value, reducing sensor error. Drift correction compensates for the difference between the estimated and actual position and orientation, reducing estimation error.
  Q How does sense-organ tracking support a display attached to a user's sense organ, and when might eye tracking be added to head tracking in virtual reality?  [16/S1/F1, 16/S1/F2, 16/S1/F3; sense-organ tracking, sense organ, display, body-controlled degree of freedom, virtual world, user experience, head tracking, eye tracking, rendering, display technology, eye movement]
    A Sense organs such as eyes and ears have body-controlled degrees of freedom, and a display attached to them must be perceived as attached to the surrounding world, so their position and orientation are tracked to update the virtual world or user experience. In virtual reality, sense-organ tracking mostly focuses on head tracking, which is sufficient for the visual and aural components related to the eyes and ears. Eye tracking may be required if rendering and display technology require compensating for eye movement, and eye tracking may be part of head tracking.
