Non-immersive and semi-immersive systems are common and cost-effective, while fully immersive systems are the costliest and rarest because they completely block all senses.
  - Immersion in VR means the user's senses are blocked from the real world so they only receive input from the virtual world.

  - Semi-immersive systems provide partial immersion by blocking some senses while leaving the user free to sense the real world.
  - Fully immersive systems completely block all senses but are the costliest and rarest systems.

2/A10  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (10 facts, 37 concepts)
  Q Why can an immersive experience that is perceived as real be useful in both healthcare and travel and tourism?  [2/S0/F3, 2/S0/F6, 1/S1/F2; immersive experience, virtual reality, mixed reality, healthcare, travel and tourism, virtual tourism]
    A An immersive experience appears real because the user perceives it as the real environment. In healthcare, virtual reality and mixed reality simulations let potential doctors explore body organs, see their parts, and learn when cadavers are difficult to obtain. In travel and tourism, virtual tours let users visit faraway places without physically going there, saving money and physical effort.
  Q Why are most commercial immersive applications semi-immersive rather than fully immersive?  [2/S1/F5, 2/S1/F7, 2/S1/F10; semi-immersive virtual reality system, fully immersive virtual reality system, immersion]
    A A semi-immersive virtual reality system gives a partial immersive experience by blocking some but not all senses. It is the most cost-effective and commonly used form of virtual reality, so most commercial immersive applications are semi-immersive. A fully immersive virtual reality system is the costliest and commercially rare because it is difficult to afford.
  Q How does a non-immersive virtual reality system differ from a semi-immersive virtual reality system in the user's sensory connection to the real world?  [2/S1/F2, 2/S1/F3, 2/S1/F5; non-immersive virtual reality system, semi-immersive virtual reality system, virtual environment, immersion]
    A A non-immersive virtual reality system is a specific, limited class of computer application in which the user controls characters or activities while retaining control of the physical environment, and the virtual environment does not directly interact with the user. The user may be psychologically engaged while the eyes, ears, and other sensory organs remain open, so the user remains physically attached to the real world. A semi-immersive virtual reality system instead blocks some but not all senses, for example the eyes, while other senses remain exposed to real-world signals.
  Q Why do virtual boot camp and combat training systems use motion trackers, full-body tracking systems, and wireless weapon controllers in addition to head-mounted displays?  [2/S0/F9, 2/S1/F9; virtual boot camp, combat training, head-mounted display, motion tracker, full-body tracking system, wireless weapon controller, fully immersive virtual reality system]
    A Defense training includes virtual boot camp and combat training that simulate vehicles, soldiers, and combat environments with constraints. Such training uses head-mounted displays with motion trackers, full-body tracking systems, and wireless weapon controllers. Fully immersive virtual reality systems detect actual movements, reactions, and even a blink of an eye and translate them into actions in the virtual world.

3/A15  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (14 facts, 22 concepts)
  Q How does augmented reality differ from virtual reality in the kind of experience it aims to provide to the user?  [3/S0/F1, 3/S3/F7, 1/S1/F2; augmented reality, virtual reality, immersive experience]
    A Augmented reality does not require an immersive experience and instead emphasizes augmenting real-world content with digital content. Virtual reality applications are characterized as immersive, and an immersive experience is one perceived through the senses as real rather than distinguishable from the user's real environment. Thus augmented reality adds digital content to the real-world view, whereas virtual reality applications aim to provide an immersive experience.
  Q Why can markerless augmented reality techniques be useful for augmented reality systems that would otherwise rely on physical markers?  [3/S2/F2, 3/S2/F4, 3/S3/F2, 3/S3/F3, 3/S3/F4; marker-based augmented reality system, markerless augmented reality technique, marker, computer vision]
    A Marker-based augmented reality systems use markers placed in the environment, and those markers are prone to damage, making the systems difficult to maintain unless the environment is very sanitized. A damaged marker may make the application not work, especially in outdoor or less well-attended settings. Markerless augmented reality techniques overcome this maintenance difficulty by not requiring physical markers and by relying primarily on computer vision or other sensors such as GPS.
  Q How do augmented reality applications in commerce and retail connect to the metaverse idea in extended reality?  [3/S0/F0, 3/S0/F2, 3/S0/F5, 1/S3/F4; extended reality, augmented reality, application domain, metaverse]
    A Extended reality technologies have application potential in domains such as retail, and augmented reality has applications in commerce, particularly e-commerce. The presence of many augmented reality applications in commerce, particularly e-commerce, is one motivation for the metaverse concept. The metaverse has heavily stimulated growth in the extended reality field, so these commerce-oriented augmented reality applications help show why the metaverse idea became relevant.
  Q How do location-based and superimposition-based markerless augmented reality applications use different sensor or recognition methods to add digital content to the real world?  [3/S2/F6, 3/S2/F7, 3/S3/F4; location-based markerless augmented reality application, superimposition-based markerless augmented reality application, location sensor, global positioning system, computer vision technique]
    A Markerless augmented reality techniques can rely primarily on computer vision, and they may also rely on sensors such as GPS in location-based applications. Location-based markerless augmented reality applications rely on a location sensor such as GPS to detect the user's location with respect to a reference frame and augment digital content when the user is at a specific location. Superimposition-based markerless augmented reality applications recognize one or multiple physical objects using computer vision techniques and superimpose digital content on the real-world scene depending on the recognized objects.

===== lecture 5: Definitions

5/A0  recall  lecture  "Before you start: recall"  (4 facts, 4 concepts)
  What is augmented reality (AR)? (lecture 1) -> Augmented reality on the reality-virtuality continuum is the intermediate state in which the real environment is augmented with virtual content.
  What is mixed reality (MR)? (lecture 1) -> Mixed reality, in a broad sense, is the entire space between the real environment extreme and the virtual environment extreme of the reality-virtuality continuum, containing different degrees of augmentation of the real environment with virtual content and of the virtual environment with real content.
  What is reality-virtuality continuum? (lecture 1) -> The reality-virtuality continuum is a scale whose extremes are a completely real environment with nothing virtual and a completely virtual environment with nothing real.
  What is virtual reality (VR)? (lecture 4) -> Virtual reality (VR) is, according to the Miriam Webster dictionary, an artificial environment experienced through sensory stimuli, in which sights and sounds play a primary role and are provided by a computer, possibly along with other sensory stimuli, and in which a person's actions partially determine what happens in the environment.

5/A14  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (12 facts, 11 concepts)
  Q How do augmented reality and virtual reality differ in their relationship to the physical environment being experienced?  [5/S1/F0, 5/S1/F1; augmented reality, virtual reality, physical environment, virtual environment]
    A Augmented reality alters the ongoing perception of the real world, whereas virtual reality completely replaces the user's sense of reality with a virtual environment. A virtual reality system does not require the physical existence of an object or place, because it creates a virtual environment, whereas an augmented reality system requires the place being experienced to exist.
  Q Why is it not enough for a system to overlay digital content on a real-world view in order to be an augmented reality system?  [5/S0/F0, 5/S0/F1, 5/S0/F2, 5/S0/F3; augmented reality, augmented reality system, real time, three-dimensional, sensory organ, interaction]
    A The dictionary definition describes augmented reality as overlaying digital information on an image of something viewed through a device. The systemic definition requires an augmented reality system to combine the real world with virtual content, be interactive in real time, and be registered in 3D. The four-characteristic identification test requires the enhancement to be registered in 3D, experienced through sensory organs, and supported by interaction in the enhanced environment, and a system is an augmented reality system only if all four characteristics are satisfied.
  Q How do definitions of mixed reality relate to augmented reality and the reality-virtuality continuum, and why does the course use mixed reality and augmented reality interchangeably?  [5/S2/F0, 5/S2/F2, 5/S2/F5, 5/S2/F6, 5/S3/F0, 1/S2/F3; mixed reality, augmented reality, reality-virtuality continuum]
    A There is no agreement on a precise definition of mixed reality. In a broad sense, mixed reality is the entire space between the real environment extreme and the virtual environment extreme of the reality-virtuality continuum, and one definition says it comprises everything in between those extremes, so augmented reality is included. Another definition treats mixed reality as a synonym for augmented reality. Because the definitions are confusing, the course assumes mixed reality is the same technology and idea as augmented reality and treats the terms as interchangeable.

  - David Brewster's 1860 compact stereoscope was an advancement toward practical immersive experiences.
  - Edwin A. Link's 1928 flight simulator was a precursor to modern multi-sensory immersive virtual reality.

6/A20  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (11 facts, 30 concepts)
  Q How does Sir Charles Wheatstone's stereoscopic demonstration connect to the way a VR headset creates immersion?  [6/S1/F1, 6/S1/F2; Sir Charles Wheatstone, stereoscopic device, virtual reality, headset, stereoscopic vision, immersion]
    A Sir Charles Wheatstone's 1838 stereoscopic device established that the human brain can process two-dimensional images as three-dimensional objects. In VR, headsets present two-dimensional images, but stereoscopic vision makes the user perceive them as three-dimensional objects and space, and that perception is immersion.
  Q Why can Sensorama be treated as the first immersive virtual reality system under the modern definition of virtual reality?  [6/S2/F3, 6/S2/F4, 1/S1/F2; Morton Heilig, Sensorama, immersive virtual reality system, virtual reality, immersive experience, three-dimensional, stereo sound, haptics]
    A Morton Heilig introduced Sensorama in 1962, and it can be considered the first immersive virtual reality system under the modern definition of virtual reality. It was publicized as having 3D, wide vision, motion, color, stereo sound, smell, wind, and vibrations, and it stimulated all sensory organs. An immersive experience is a perceived experience through sensory organs that appears real because the person cannot distinguish the environment from the real environment, so multi-sensory stimulation like Sensorama's fits that definition.
  Q How did the availability of inexpensive headsets such as the Oculus Rift help VR move from the end of its modern phase into its current growth phase?  [6/S3/F6, 6/S3/F7, 6/S3/F8, 6/S4/F0; Oculus Rift, Palmer Luckey, Facebook, virtual reality, headset, modern phase, current growth phase, virtual reality headset, immersive experience]
    A The Oculus Rift was an inexpensive VR headset created by Palmer Luckey in 2012, and Facebook's purchase of it in 2014 helped try to popularize the headset and make VR affordable. The availability of inexpensive headsets such as the Oculus Rift greatly spurred interest in VR towards the end of its modern phase. The growth phase then began roughly in 2016, when many well-known companies released commercial VR headsets that made immersive experiences affordable to a large population.
  Q How do the 2016 commercial headset releases and the 2021 popularization of the metaverse connect within the current growth phase?  [6/S4/F0, 6/S4/F2, 1/S3/F4; current growth phase, virtual reality headset, metaverse, Zuckerberg, Meta, immersive experience]
    A The current growth phase began roughly in 2016 when many well-known companies released commercial virtual reality headsets, making immersive experiences affordable to a large population and spurring growth in the field. The metaverse term had been in use for more than 30 years and had existed in the research community since the 1990s, but in 2021 Zuckerberg of Meta popularized it. After that, people began taking an interest in the metaverse and interpreting it in different ways, which spurred further activity.

7/A20  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (9 facts, 39 concepts)
  Q How does the historical framing of augmented reality differ from the historical framing of virtual reality's formative years?  [7/S0/F0, 6/S2/F0; augmented reality, virtual reality, experimental phase, basic research phase, tools or applications phase, commercial application phase]
    A The formative years of virtual reality are described as roughly 1950 to 1990, ending with the popularization of the term virtual reality in the late 1980s. In contrast, the historical evolution of augmented reality is divided into four phases that may sometimes overlap. Those AR phases are the experimental phase before the 1980s, the basic research phase from the 1980s to the mid-1990s, the tools or applications phase from the mid-1990s to 2007, and the commercial application phase from 2007 to the present.
  Q What does the Sutherland-Sproull prototype show about how an early augmented reality system could satisfy the systemic requirements of combining real and virtual content and being registered in 3D?  [7/S1/F0, 7/S1/F1, 5/S0/F1; Sutherland-Sproull augmented reality prototype, Ivan Sutherland, Bob Sproull, optical see-through head-mounted display, mechanical tracking system, three-dimensional registration, augmented reality system]
    A Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull created the first truly computer-generated augmented reality prototype in 1968, using a CRT-based optical see-through head-mounted display and a ceiling-mounted mechanical tracking system connected to a PDP-11 computer and custom graphics hardware. The tracker registered head movements in three dimensions on the see-through display. The systemic definition of an augmented reality system says that it combines the real world with virtual content and is registered in 3D, and the prototype's see-through HMD with 3D head registration corresponds to those characteristics.
  Q What connection can be drawn between early handheld augmented reality systems such as NaviCam and the later commercial growth of mobile augmented reality?  [7/S2/F6, 7/S2/F7, 7/S4/F2, 7/S4/F3; NaviCam, mobile augmented reality system, smartphone-based augmented reality, ARKit, ARCore]
    A NaviCam was a 1995 handheld or mobile augmented reality system that superimposed virtual tags on real objects using handheld and head-mounted displays. Early handheld systems like NaviCam were true augmented reality applications and precursors to the kind of AR applications seen today. In 2009, augmented reality moved to widely available commercial technology partly because of smartphone-based augmented reality, and Apple's ARKit and Google's ARCore further stimulated growth by enabling many developers to create augmented reality applications.

  What is virtual reality (VR)? (lecture 4) -> Virtual reality (VR) is, according to the Miriam Webster dictionary, an artificial environment experienced through sensory stimuli, in which sights and sounds play a primary role and are provided by a computer, possibly along with other sensory stimuli, and in which a person's actions partially determine what happens in the environment.

(lecture 4) -> A virtual reality (VR) system is defined by three characteristics: an artificial or synthetic environment, sensory experience through computer-generated stimuli, and user actions that partially determine what happens in the environment; a system with these aspects can be categorized as a virtual reality system and distinguished from other systems, which are not supposed to have these aspects.

8/A16  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (17 facts, 28 concepts)
  Q Why are immersion and presence treated as different kinds of issues in virtual reality, and what does that mean for what a virtual reality system must provide to achieve its overall goal?  [8/S0/F1, 8/S1/F7, 8/S3/F0, 8/S3/F1, 8/S3/F2, 8/S3/F4, 8/S3/F5; virtual reality, immersion, presence, sensory stimuli, virtual reality system, virtual environment]
    A Virtual reality involves two major issues: the technological issue of generating suitable sensory stimuli and the psychological issue of perceiving those stimuli. Immersion corresponds to the technological side, giving an objective and quantifiable description of what a system provides, while presence is the psychological, subjective sense of being in the virtual environment. The overall goal is high presence, so a system needs high immersion to increase the potential for presence, but immersion does not ensure presence.
  Q How do the objective qualities of immersion explain the way a virtual reality system presents the artificial environment described in the definition of virtual reality?  [8/S1/F0, 8/S1/F1, 8/S1/F2, 8/S1/F3, 8/S1/F4, 8/S1/F5, 8/S1/F6, 4/S0/F2, 4/S1/F0; virtual reality, virtual reality system, artificial environment, sensory stimuli, immersion, extensive, matching, surrounding, vivid, interactivity, plot-informing]
    A A virtual reality system is an artificial or synthetic environment in which computer-generated sensory stimuli provide the experience and user actions partially determine what happens. Immersion is the objective degree to which such a system projects stimuli, and its qualities name the dimensions of that projection. Extensive and vivid describe the range and energy of the sensory modalities, surrounding makes cues panoramic, matching and interactivity connect stimuli to user movement and action, and plot-informing makes the world's behavior understandable.
  Q How does the description of immersion as blocking real-world input relate to the objective qualities used to evaluate a virtual reality system's stimulus projection?  [8/S1/F0, 8/S1/F1, 8/S1/F3, 8/S1/F4, 2/S1/F0; immersion, sensory stimuli, virtual world, extensive, surrounding, vivid, field of view, resolution, lighting, frame rate, audio bit rate]
    A Immersion can be described as blocking real-world input so that the user receives input only from the virtual world. The objective qualities describe how the system projects that virtual input. Extensive covers more sensory modalities, surrounding makes cues more panoramic through a wider field of view, and vivid increases stimulus energy through factors such as resolution, lighting, frame rate, or audio bit rate.

16/A15  open_question  lecture  "Think it through"  (15 facts, 47 concepts)
  Q How do pose estimation and environment tracking together support the registration of digital content in augmented reality?  [1/S2/F1, 9/S3/F5, 16/S0/F3, 16/S1/F8, 16/S1/F7; augmented reality, pose, anchor, environment tracking, digital content, physical object, three-dimensional space, field of view, real-world view, virtual reality]
    A Augmented reality is the intermediate state in which the real environment is augmented with virtual content. To register virtual content in augmented reality, the pose of the viewer relative to an anchor in the real world must be determined, because without proper pose estimation three-dimensional registration is difficult or impossible. In augmented reality, tracking also requires moving real-world objects in the field of view, and a moved physical object must be tracked in the real-world view so that digital content can be appropriately registered. Environment tracking is more important in augmented reality for this purpose, although it is also important in virtual reality.
  Q How does the position of a sensor determine the two broad ways to obtain tracking input, and why does that affect where outside-in tracking can work?  [16/S2/F1, 16/S2/F2, 16/S2/F4, 16/S2/F5; tracking, tracker position, outside-in tracking, inside-out tracking, external sensor, sensor range, head-mounted display, pose, motion]
    A Tracking is categorized based on the position of the tracker or sensor into outside-in and inside-out tracking. In outside-in tracking, the experience device does not contain the sensors; external sensors such as cameras or markers provide data to identify and track motion, pose, and orientation. Outside-in tracking works only within the sensor range where those external sensors are placed. Inside-out tracking locates cameras or other sensors within the experience device, such as a head-mounted display, so no external devices are needed and there is no such placement restriction.
  Q What different errors are reduced by calibration and drift correction in the tracking process?  [16/S3/F1, 16/S3/F2, 16/S3/F5; tracking, tracking process, calibration, drift correction, sensor error, estimation error, position, orientation]
    A The tracking process broadly consists of calibration, registration, integration, and drift correction. Calibration transforms the output of imperfect sensors so that it behaves as closely as possible to the natural or real value, reducing sensor error. Drift correction compensates for the difference between the estimated and actual position and orientation, reducing estimation error.
  Q How does sense-organ tracking support a display attached to a user's sense organ, and when might eye tracking be added to head tracking in virtual reality?  [16/S1/F1, 16/S1/F2, 16/S1/F3; sense-organ tracking, sense organ, display, body-controlled degree of freedom, virtual world, user experience, head tracking, eye tracking, rendering, display technology, eye movement]
    A Sense organs such as eyes and ears have body-controlled degrees of freedom, and a display attached to them must be perceived as attached to the surrounding world, so their position and orientation are tracked to update the virtual world or user experience. In virtual reality, sense-organ tracking mostly focuses on head tracking, which is sufficient for the visual and aural components related to the eyes and ears. Eye tracking may be required if rendering and display technology require compensating for eye movement, and eye tracking may be part of head tracking.
