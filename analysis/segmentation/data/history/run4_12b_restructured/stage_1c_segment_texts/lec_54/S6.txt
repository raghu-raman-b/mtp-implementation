[54/204] Light emitted from pixel elements after excitation starts decaying over time.
[54/205] That is the physical property of the material which makes the pixels.
[54/206] Now, this decaying light leads to fading of the scene after some time.
[54/207] Although the image is not changing, the scene starts fading because the emitted light starts decaying over time.
[54/208] Pixels in a scene may get excited at different points in time and hence may not fade in sync, which may lead to image distortion.
[54/209] So, when we are rendering on a raster scan device, we are following a left-to-right, top-to-bottom manner.
[54/210] So, excitation of the top-left pixel is done much ahead of the excitation of the bottom-right-most pixel.
[54/211] By the time the bottom-right-most pixel is excited, the top-left pixel may start decaying, and this may happen with other pixels as well.
[54/212] So, correspondingly, the image that we finally get to see may not appear uniform and may appear distorted.
[54/213] That is another issue with this decaying.
[54/214] One is the scene starts fading; the second one is the scene may not be rendered without distortion.
[54/215] To avoid this, what is typically done is to keep on exciting the pixels periodically.
[54/216] This is called refreshing.
[54/217] So, although the image is not changing, the scanning mechanism still happens periodically so that we get to see uniformity in the image rendered.
[54/218] The refresh rate is the number of times a scene is refreshed per second on the screen.
[54/219] It is typically represented in terms of hertz, and a good refresh rate for most devices is 60 hertz.
[54/220] However, as we noted earlier in one of our discussions on human factors, a higher refresh rate is always preferable for virtual reality because here the screen is much closer to the eyes as compared to typical computer usage where the screen and eye are reasonably at a distance.
[54/221] So, a higher refresh rate is always preferable for a VR experience, a better VR experience.
