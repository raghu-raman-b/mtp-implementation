[46/11] So, just to recollect, when we talk of research questions, essentially this is the question whose answers we seek during an empirical study.
[46/12] In empirical study, generally we conduct experiments, collect data, and analyze data to find out answers to the questions that we formulate at this stage.
[46/13] Now, there are two types of questions if you may recollect.
[46/14] One is testable, the other is untestable.
[46/15] Testable questions are specific in nature.
[46/16] They do not generally give us; the answers to those questions do not generally give us conclusions about the overall usability of the system, or generalizability of the answer.
[46/17] Whereas untestable are the more generic form.
[46/18] The answer is relevant to a general conclusion on usability.
[46/19] It is possible to conduct experiments and collect data for testable questions, whereas it is not possible to conduct experiments and collect data for untestable questions.
[46/20] So, there is always a trade-off.
[46/21] To balance the trade-off, generally what happens is that to seek answers to the usability concerns, we frame multiple testable questions, conduct experiments, collect data, and analyze data to come to conclusions about those testable questions, and on the basis of those conclusions for multiple testable questions, we come to a conclusion on the broader untestable usability-related questions.
[46/22] Such questions can be: untestable questions can be: is the system usable, is the system good, is the system user-friendly, and so on.
[46/23] Whereas testable questions can be: examples of testable questions can be: does my system provide a lower error rate in the first two hours of usage?
[46/24] That is about research questions.
