[43/49] Now, in order to perform the quick and cheap evaluation, we require two things.
[43/50] First is at least a low-fidelity prototype; that is, higher quality prototypes are better, but at least we require a low-fidelity prototype and a team of evaluators.
[43/51] Of course, that is quite obvious because without evaluators, we cannot evaluate anything.
[43/52] Now, this evaluation team is unique.
[43/53] It may be the design team itself or may include other skilled designers who have some knowledge and skill in the domain of the problem.
[43/54] Optionally, few end users may also be included.
[43/55] Note that here we are testing usability.
[43/56] The primary condition of usability testing is that we must involve the users.
[43/57] However, in the curious case of quick evaluation, Involvement of end users may be optional.
[43/58] Primarily we engage design team members or people familiar with the design problem as evaluators.
[43/59] Why so?
[43/60] Because we are assuming that they are the experts in the domain of the problem and they have as good experience as a user.
[43/61] So their feedback is likely to reflect that of the users.
[43/62] Now since identifying And engaging design team members is much, much less complicated as compared to engaging a full-fledged evaluation team involving end users.
[43/63] So, it is likely to be quick and cheap.
[43/64] Now, the evaluation team should have at least three to five members.
[43/65] More is, of course, always welcome, but three to five should be sufficient.
[43/66] Now, each team member evaluates individually and produces a report.
[43/67] So, the prototype is presented to each evaluator, each evaluator evaluates it independently and produces a report.
[43/68] The report contains a list of usability issues as perceived by the evaluator.
[43/69] At the end, all these reports are combined to produce a final list of usability issues.
[43/70] Now, this combination, of course, is done by the design team, or the lead of the team.
