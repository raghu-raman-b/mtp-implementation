So, first let us start with the basics of sound. Most of us may already be aware of it, but like before, it is always better to recap our basic knowledge. So that subsequent discussions are understandable. Now, what is sound? Sound is nothing but vibration in a medium and that medium is usually air, but could also be some other medium such as water or any other gases, liquids, and solids.
So, it requires some medium and Sound always propagates through a medium unlike light which may propagate without any medium as well. So, there is no sound in a vacuum unlike light propagation which can propagate through vacuum. Now, molecules in the medium get displaced causing variation in pressure which ranges from compression extreme, that is when the pressure is extremely high, to decompression extreme or decompressed, which means the pressure is very low; that is also called rarefaction extreme. So, essentially when sound is generated, that creates pressure differences in different parts of the medium. ranging from compression extreme to decompression extreme, and that is the mechanism which allows sound to propagate through the medium. So, at a fixed point in space, that is within the medium, pressure varies as a function of time. The same is applicable in the case of the human eardrum.
Pressure variation can occur on a human eardrum which is converted into a perceptual experience that we consider to be auditory experience. Now this brings us to the representation of sound for better understanding. So we can represent sound in the form of a sound wave. So which is nothing but a plot of pressure variation against time. So as you can see in this accompanying figure, the x-axis represents time, the y-axis represents air pressure.
So as time progresses, the pressure varies from one extreme to another and it goes on like this, like a sinusoidal wave. And that representation we can consider to be representing sound. So, since we can represent sound as a wave, we can utilize wave properties to characterize sound. So, properties of sound as a wave, there are many such properties that we can utilize for various purposes. So, the first thing is frequency.
Like a wave, sound can also be considered to have a frequency, which is the number of alterations between one maximum to another maximum per second, which is measured in Hertz. The unit is Hertz. Now, the human ear is designed to hear only a range of frequencies generated by sound waves, which varies from 20 Hertz to 20,000 Hertz. Anything between this range is audible to a human ear and anything outside this range is not audible to a human ear. However, that is the limitation of human physiology, but other animals can perceive a higher range outside this audible range of humans. So, that is frequency. Another property is amplitude. which is like any other wave, which is defined as the maximum displacement from the mean or rest position of the wave.
It is measured in dB or decibel and measured relative to the minimum sound detectable by humans at 1000 Hz. So, this is the second property amplitude. Third is phase. It is the start position of a cycle, a cycle of the wave, where it can begin at 0 to 360 degrees and is measured in degrees. So, we have primarily these three properties that are relevant for dealing with sound waves: frequency, amplitude, and phase.

So, the human hearing system contains three parts. Now, what are these three parts? Outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear.
So, we have three parts of the human hearing system: outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear. This part we can call the outer ear. Middle ear here and inner ear here. So, let us start with the outer ear. There are a lot of components in our auditory system.
A crucial one is the canal. This canal works as an amplifier, boosting the sound pressure for a range of frequencies. So, this is the receiver part, and then it receives the wave, then forces it to propagate through the canal which amplifies the wave, which is part of the outer ear. And because of the kind of folds that are there, the complex folds attenuate high frequencies. So, if there are some high frequency sound waves appearing, then due to the design of our canal and the receiver, the high frequencies get attenuated.
Then comes the middle ear. So, once the wave passes through this canal at the outer ear, it then strikes the tympanic membrane causing vibration. This is the tympanic membrane. So, the wave hits this membrane causing the membrane to vibrate. Now, this vibration then transfers to three bones which are part of the middle ear.
Also known as ossicles that are housed in an air-filled chamber, namely malleus, incus and stapes. So, these three are shown here: malleus, incus and stapes. So, these three bones are part of the middle ear. And finally, we have the inner ear. Now, this is the size of a marble which houses a component or an organ known as cochlea which is the sense organ for hearing.
This is the cochlea. This is part of the inner ear. Now, it converts sound energy to neural impulses. So, the cochlea is connected with nerves or neurons. And it converts the sound energy, that is the wave that is coming to it through the various mechanisms, to neural impulses to be transmitted to the brain through the neural network.
