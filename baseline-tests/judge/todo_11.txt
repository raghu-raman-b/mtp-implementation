## M09 [multihop, expect yes] What earlier ideas does the resource optimisation of the Majuli app for the Quest 2 and phones rest on?
GOLD: The hardware lecture says a VR system's processing element is typically a PC or a high-end workstation with a good GPU, useful for rendering complex scenes (lecture 14). The human factors lecture says VR needs frame rates much higher than the 24 or 60 fps of television and film, and the resource lecture notes a higher refresh rate is preferable (lectures 28, 54); VR is resource hungry and the aim is a good experience on affordable hardware (lecture 54). Interactive VR redraws the scene with every input and anti-aliasing adds further computation, so rendering cost grows manifold and GPUs exploit the parallelism (lecture 58). Hence the Quest 2, with less compute and storage than a workstation and about 10 GB of rich content, needed cloud storage for videos (with bandwidth, coverage and latency demands), and phones needed compressed images, no video and in extreme cases removed features (lecture 59).

## M10 [multihop, expect yes] How are presence and cybersickness related in this course, and when does reducing one cost the other?
GOLD: Control over motion affects presence directly and cybersickness indirectly through presence; greater presence generally leads to lower cybersickness, though not always (lecture 11). A sensory mismatch can cause cybersickness for some users, and when the user consciously notices the conflict presence is compromised (lecture 26). Mitigation is a trade-off: restricting the field of view reduces cybersickness but can reduce presence (lecture 12), so mitigation must avoid compromising presence (lecture 49). The Majuli evaluation measured both, presence with the QEP questionnaire and cybersickness with the SSQ before and after exposure, finding good presence but significant changes in oculomotor and total sickness (lecture 47).

## M12 [multihop, expect yes] Why might adding vibration or motion cues to a VR setup reduce cybersickness?
GOLD: Motion sickness occurs when the brain receives conflicting signals from different senses, with symptoms similar to cybersickness (lecture 10). In vection, vision reports that the user is moving or accelerating while the balance sense and proprioception say the user is motionless, and the goal should be to balance the experience across senses (lecture 26). The mitigation lecture lists hardware-level alterations that add matching cues: phantom legs (haptic cues synchronised to footsteps, reduced cybersickness to a great extent), floor vibrations matching the virtual environment, sound with vibration, and modified motion platforms where short bursts of rotational acceleration matched visual forward acceleration and were quite effective (lecture 12). The hardware lecture presents haptics and motion platforms, which move the whole body to give a sense of physical motion and gravity, as VR output devices (lecture 14).
-- ours_v2 r1: Adding vibration or motion cues can help reduce cybersickness by providing hardware-level alterations that provide tactile feedback. For example, a walking vibe system uses two small vibration motors behind the ears to provide vibrotactile feedback during a walking experience. Additionally, floor vibrations can be used as a hardware-level alteration to provide vibration-driven whole-body tactile feedback that matches the vibrations of the virtual environment (lecture 12, 23:02).
