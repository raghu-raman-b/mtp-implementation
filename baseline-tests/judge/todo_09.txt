## M01 [multihop, expect yes] Why do the cybersickness-reduction techniques that restrict the field of view work on the edges of the view rather than the centre?
GOLD: Peripheral vision is better than central vision at detecting movement (lecture 27). Vection, the sensation of self-motion, is highest when motion is sensed in peripheral stimuli and correlates positively with visual motion stimulus, so visual motion plays a crucial role in the onset and severity of cybersickness (lecture 11). Restricting the field of view therefore reduces peripheral optical flow and ideally reduces cybersickness; an asymmetric mask obscures only peripheral regions with higher optical flow during locomotion and leaves low-flow regions visible (lecture 12). The cost is that field-of-view restriction can reduce presence and impair visual tasks (lecture 12).

## M02 [multihop, expect yes] Why did the Majuli VR tour move users between places with teleportation and a vehicle instead of letting them walk everywhere?
GOLD: Free walking is the most natural travel technique but completely free walking is not practical; full-gait walking is confined to the physical space and mapped to a potentially infinite virtual space, with a mismatch (lecture 40). Teleportation, an instantaneous transition, is found to reduce cybersickness compared with naturalistic walking, and teleportation instead of steering is a listed locomotion modification for cybersickness (lectures 11, 12). In the Majuli design the island's geographic spread is large and content rich, so exploring it by walking takes a lot of time and may lead to cybersickness; a virtual vehicle metaphor (a pre-planned route, the user selects a destination) was added for long-distance travel, with controlled joystick navigation without abrupt movements, while the vehicle's viewpoint speed must be chosen carefully (lecture 41). Teleportation gives the shortest exposure but is not natural (lecture 51), so the tour combines walking within each information point with teleportation or the vehicle between points because one technique alone would not suit the large spread (lecture 59).

## M03 [multihop, expect yes] How does the finding that longer VR exposure makes people sicker turn into a method for planning a tour route?
GOLD: Prolonged exposure through HMDs leads to greater cybersickness (lecture 11), and users are advised to avoid long continuous exposure, with roughly 55 to 70 minutes suggested as generally safe (lecture 12). The optimisation lectures therefore try to control exposure duration, which depends on world size, travelling speed, travel time and interaction time (lecture 51). The first approach represents the tour as a graph of points of interest and finds the path covering all of them in minimum time with a travelling salesman formulation using a natural walking-speed model (lecture 51). If that minimum still exceeds the time threshold above which cybersickness may occur (walking a whole satra could take up to a day), a second approach assigns each point an importance and waiting time and finds the most important path within the threshold with a vehicle-routing-with-profit solver (lectures 52, 53).
-- ours_v2 r1: The finding that cybersickness (CS) increases with longer VR exposure duration is used to determine a "time threshold" for tour planning (lecture 52, 0:40; lecture 11, 14:44). 

To plan the Majuli virtual reality tour route, this information is applied as follows:
* **Time Limit Constraint**: Because exploring a large geographical area takes time, a time limit is established based on the threshold beyond which cybersickness occurs (lecture 41, 2:07; lecture 51, 41:39).
* **Path Optimization**: Instead of simply finding the minimum time to visit every location, the tour is designed to find an optimal path that lets a user visit as many important points of interest as possible while keeping the total exposure time below that threshold (lecture 52, 13:09; lecture 51, 41:39).
-- ours_v2 r2: Because cybersickness is directly correlated with exposure duration—with symptoms rising proportionally as session time increases (lecture 51, 12:48; lecture 51, 3:34)—optimizing a VR tour involves managing this time. 

Specifically, planning a tour route turns into a optimization problem where the goal is to find an optimal path that satisfies two conditions (lecture 52, 13:09):
* It must keep the user's exposure below a specific time threshold beyond which cybersickness may occur.
* Within that time limit, it must allow the user to visit as many important points of interest (POIs) as possible.

A simple "minimum-time" approach that covers all points of interest might still exceed these safe time thresholds, especially in large virtual worlds, which is why a time-limited path optimization is necessary (lecture 51, 41:39; lecture 53, 1:31).

## M04 [multihop, expect yes] Which headsets, development tools and computers did the Majuli VR tour use, and how did the limits of those devices shape the app?
GOLD: The tour was built in Unity (version 2021.3.22f1) for the Oculus Quest 2 and for Android smartphones (Android 6), using the XR toolkit for the Quest version and the Google VR SDK for the Cardboard version (lecture 44); Unity is a cross-platform engine supporting over 20 platforms (lecture 60). The evaluation used a Meta Quest 2 (1920 by 1832 pixels per eye) connected to a Windows 11 PC with a GTX 1080 GPU via Oculus Link, with joystick-based controllers (lecture 47). The Quest 2 is a standalone device with its own processor and memory but less computing power and storage than a high-end graphics workstation, so all videos were stored in the cloud, giving a 5.1 GB app that needs high bandwidth, network coverage and low latency (lectures 44, 59). For smartphones in Cardboard, images were compressed and most videos removed, cutting the size from about 10 GB to 0.7 GB at the cost of realism (lectures 44, 59).
-- ours_v2 r1: The Majuli VR tour was developed using the Unity engine version 2021.3.22f1 (lecture 44, 17:44). For the different versions of the tour, the following hardware and software tools were used:
* **Oculus Quest 2**: Developed using the Extended Reality (XR) toolkit (lecture 44, 17:44) and connected to a personal computer (PC) via Oculus Link (lecture 47, 6:09). This version included both a 360-degree-image tour and a three-dimensional (3D) model tour (lecture 44, 22:21).
* **Google Cardboard**: Developed for Android smartphones (requiring Android 6.0) using the Google Virtual Reality (VR) software development kit (SDK; lecture 44, 17:44).

The limitations of these devices shaped the app in the following ways:
* **Resource Optimization**: To accommodate device limits, the system required compressing images and removing video playback support, which made the experience less realistic than the full version implementation (lecture 59, 12:08).
* **Geographic Scope**: The project faced the challenge of combining vast heritage information into a usable immersive tour that covers a large geography (nearly 427 square kilometers), which is different from typical virtual tours of smaller locations like museums (lecture 34, 19:00).
