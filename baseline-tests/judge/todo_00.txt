## D01 [definition, expect yes] what is HRTF
GOLD: Head-related transfer functions (HRTF) model how a listener receives sound from a location in space; they depend on the size and shape of the head, ears and ear canal, so every person has a unique HRTF. Because measuring each user's is impractical, an HRTF for an average listener is used. A pair of HRTFs (both ears) can synthesise binaural sound and implement localisation in an XR system.

## D02 [definition, expect yes] What is an IMU?
GOLD: An inertial measurement unit is a composite hardware unit containing mainly a gyroscope (angular velocity), an accelerometer (linear acceleration on x, y, z) and a magnetometer (magnetic field strength on x, y, z); it is part of phones and HMDs. Tracking relies mainly on the gyroscope; accelerometer and magnetometer help detect and correct drift.
-- ours_v2 r1: An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a self-contained hardware unit that measures changes in orientation, translation velocity, or acceleration rather than absolute values (lecture 17, 3:26; lecture 18, 24:30). 

It mainly contains three types of sensors (lecture 17, 3:26):
* **Gyroscope:** Captures angular velocity.
* **Accelerometer:** Captures linear acceleration along the x, y, and z axes.
* **Magnetometer:** Captures magnetic field strength along the x, y, and z axes.

IMUs are typically based on microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) and are commonly found in modern devices such as smartphones and head-mounted displays (HMDs; lecture 17, 3:26; lecture 18, 24:56). In tracking, the gyroscope's angular velocity readings are the primary source of information, while the accelerometer and magnetometer can be used to detect and correct drift errors (lecture 17, 3:26; lecture 17, 18:29).
-- ours_v2_nothink r1: An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a self-contained hardware unit used to measure changes in orientation, translation velocity, or acceleration, rather than absolute values (lecture 18, 24:30; lecture 17, 3:26). ## D04 [definition, expect yes] What is presence in VR?
GOLD: Presence is the psychological (subjective) side: a state in which part or all of a person's experience is generated by or filtered through technology but the person's perception fails to acknowledge the technology's role (International Society for Presence Research definition); the user cannot distinguish the computer-generated stimuli from real-world ones. Perfect presence is the inability to distinguish virtual from real.
-- ours_v2 r1: Presence is a subjective psychological state or sense of being in a virtual environment (lecture 8, 26:40). It occurs when a user perceives computer-generated stimuli as coming from the real world because they fail to acknowledge the role of the technology in the experience (lecture 8, 23:14).

Key aspects of presence include:
* **Psychological vs. Technological**: While immersion is considered a technological issue, presence is the corresponding psychological issue (lecture 8, 21:49; lecture 9, 23:22).
* **Relation to Immersion**: Presence can be a function of immersion; while higher immersion provides a greater potential for a user to feel present, immersion does not guarantee presence (lecture 8, 26:40).
* **Goals and Requirements**: The overall goal of a VR system is to create a high degree of presence, making users feel as if they are really in the virtual world (lecture 8, 26:40; lecture 9, 1:28). This can be achieved by fooling the user's perceptual system into believing the virtual system is the familiar real world (lecture 25, 7:27).
* **Factors**: Presence does not require photorealism, as other cues can be equally useful (lecture 9, 13:04). Additionally, developing an attachment to the experience and providing believable audio are important for increasing presence (lecture 29, 6:41).
* **Impact on Cybersickness**: Greater presence in VR generally reduces cybersickness (lecture 11, 6:48).
-- ours_v2_nothink r1: Presence is a subjective psychological state where a user perceives an experience generated by human-made technology as real (lecture 8, 23:14; lecture 8, 25:05). In this state, the user fails to acknowledge the role of the technology and perceives computer-generated stimuli as coming from the real world (lecture 8, 23:14).

Key aspects of presence include:
* **Psychological Nature**: It is a sense of being in a virtual environment that exists inside the user's mind, has no direct manifestation, and is difficult to measure objectively (lecture 8, 26:40).
* **Relation to Immersion**: While immersion is the technological issue, presence is the psychological issue (lecture 8, 21:49). A system's immersion can aid an enhanced perception of presence, but immersion does not guarantee it (lecture 8, 26:40).
* **Goals**: The primary goal of any virtual reality system is to create a high degree of presence, making users feel they are truly in the virtual world (lecture 8, 26:40; lecture 9, 1:28).

## D06 [definition, expect yes] What is vection?
GOLD: Vection is the illusion (sensation) of self-motion: a person moving in a virtual environment while physically stationary gets visual input reporting motion while the balance sense/proprioception reports none, a sensory conflict; it is highest with motion in peripheral stimuli and linked to the onset and severity of cybersickness.

It occurs when a person receives visual input reporting movement or acceleration while their balance sense or proprioception reports no motion, creating a sensory conflict (lecture 26, 20:44). Vection is highest when motion is sensed in peripheral stimuli and is positively correlated with visual motion stimulus (lecture 11, 3:39).
-- ours_v2_nothink r1: Vection is the illusion of self-motion experienced by a physically stationary person in a virtual environment. ## E02 [explanation, expect yes] How does the Wizard of Oz approach work?
GOLD: A prototyping technique: the participant believes they interact with an autonomous computer, but a hidden human operator (the wizard) produces the responses. Example: IBM's 1984 listening typewriter, where a hidden typist typed what the user spoke so the idea of speech recognition could be tested before the technology existed.
-- ours_v2 r1: The Wizard of Oz approach is a prototype-creation technique where a human subject believes they are interacting with an autonomous computer. In reality, an unseen human operator, known as the "wizard," operates the computer and provides the requested responses or results to the subject (lecture 42, 35:43).

An example of this was used in IBM's 1984 listening typewriter experiment (lecture 42, 36:40):
* A user spoke into a microphone while facing a computer they believed to be functional.
* A hidden wizard, separated by a wall, heard the speech and typed the corresponding characters.
* The characters were sent to the user's screen, making it appear that the speech was being automatically converted by the computer.

## E04 [explanation, expect yes] What is the field of view and why does it matter for cybersickness?
GOLD: FOV is the extent to which the headset display captures the user's field of vision (an angular measure of what can be seen). A larger FOV gives more immersion; reducing FOV can alleviate cybersickness, but an excessively narrow FOV lowers immersion and increases head movement, which may worsen cybersickness, so an optimal FOV is needed; FOV adjustment is a developer-side mitigation.
-- ours_v2 r1: Field of view (FOV) is an angular measure of the region visible to the eye at a single point in time (lecture 28, 11:54). In virtual reality (VR), it represents the amount of the virtual environment visible to the user, or the extent to which a headset's display captures the user's field of vision (lecture 11, 9:19; lecture 12, 16:24).

FOV matters for cybersickness (CS) because it is a content-related factor that affects susceptibility to the condition (lecture 11, 9:19; lecture 11, 1:08). Reducing the FOV can effectively alleviate CS symptoms because it restricts peripheral optical flow (lecture 11, 10:03; lecture 12, 16:42). However, an excessively narrow FOV can also increase cybersickness by decreasing immersion and requiring more head movement to see the environment (lecture 11, 10:18).
-- ours_v2_nothink r1: Field of view (FOV) is an angular measure of the region visible to the eye at a single point in time (lecture 28, 11:54). In the context of virtual reality, it refers to the amount of the virtual environment that is visible to the user at any given moment (lecture 12, 16:24).

FOV matters for cybersickness because:
* **Susceptibility:** FOV is a content-related factor that affects a user's susceptibility to cybersickness (lecture 11, 9:19).
* **Mitigation:** Reducing the FOV can be an effective way to alleviate cybersickness symptoms because it restricts peripheral optical flow (lecture 11, 10:03; lecture 12, 16:42).
* **Trade-offs:** While a larger FOV generally results in greater immersion (lecture 11, 9:45), an excessively narrow FOV can decrease immersion and may increase head movement to see around the environment, which can exacerbate cybersickness (lecture 11, 10:18).

Furthermore, the main objective of VR is to let the user experience an artificial environment through sensory stimuli for all sensory organs, rather than just providing a tool for design (lecture 8, 5:16).

* A virtual or synthetic environment (lecture 4, 6:01)

* Interactive user actions that partially determine what happens in the environment (lecture 4, 11:20)
