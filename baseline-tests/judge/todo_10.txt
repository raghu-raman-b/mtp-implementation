## M05 [multihop, expect yes] What different ways can an XR system track the user, and what are the strengths and weaknesses of each?
GOLD: Head tracking is the most important thing to track in VR (lecture 13); full-body tracking can use several surrounding cameras that see reflective markers on a suit (lecture 14). By sensor position, outside-in tracking uses external sensors or markers and is restricted to the area where they are placed, whereas inside-out tracking carries the sensors with the user and works anywhere (lecture 16). Inertial tracking in an IMU relies mainly on the gyroscope, which drifts, so drift correction needs at least two sensors blended, for example a gyroscope with a camera, accelerometer or magnetometer using a gain parameter alpha (lecture 17). Other tracker families are electromechanical, electromagnetic, acoustic, vision-based (infrared tracking is unaffected by poor lighting and shadows), inertial and hybrid; SLAM gives drift-free tracking of unknown environments, and GPS tracks position outdoors but is less accurate indoors (lecture 18).

## M06 [multihop, expect yes] What can developers, designers and users do to reduce cybersickness, from the headset to the design of a whole tour?
GOLD: Developer-side strategies are field-of-view adjustment (which reduces peripheral optical flow but can reduce presence), dynamic visual blur, rest frames, optical-flow manipulation, hardware-level alterations such as phantom legs, floor vibrations and modified motion platforms, and locomotion changes such as teleportation instead of steering; users can limit continuous exposure (about 55 to 70 minutes), habituate with repeated short exposures, use slow diaphragmatic breathing, and use pleasant music and air flow (lecture 12). The cybersickness guidelines are VR-specific design guidelines and may not apply to AR (lecture 38). The Majuli design used controlled joystick navigation without abrupt movement and a vehicle metaphor whose viewpoint speed must be chosen carefully (lecture 41). The optimisation lectures reduce exposure duration with a minimum-time path and, if that exceeds the cybersickness time threshold, a most-important-points path within the threshold (lectures 51, 52, 53).
-- ours_v2 r1: To reduce cybersickness, actions can be taken at the hardware, content, and tour-design levels:

**Hardware and Technical Factors**
* **Latency:** Reducing latency is crucial, as cybersickness increases as latency increases in head-mounted displays (HMDs; lecture 10, 20:36).
* **Hardware Alterations:** Cybersickness can be reduced or eliminated through hardware-level alterations, which include modifying existing hardware or proposing new hardware (lecture 12, 23:02).

**Content and Design Factors**
* **Field of View (FOV):** Reducing the field of view in VR is an effective way to alleviate cybersickness symptoms (lecture 11, 10:03).
* **Movement and Navigation:** Cybersickness is minimized by reducing the distance covered during navigation or the duration of exposure to the virtual world (lecture 49, 12:28). Shorter exposure durations can help minimize or eliminate the onset of symptoms (lecture 51, 3:34).
* **Tour/Content Organization:** For large areas, such as the Majuli virtual reality tour, cybersickness challenges can be addressed by allowing users to access only subsets of data based on their specific needs (lecture 37, 16:12).

**Note on Trade-offs**
* Mitigation strategies often come at a cost to "presence," as reducing cybersickness may make the environment feel more artificial and less immersive (lecture 49, 11:14).

The evaluation uses the empirical-study process of research question, variables, controlled experiment (lecture 45) and experiment design with representative participants, 12 to 25 generally being enough (lecture 46).
